## Executive Summary

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo is a 13 km hill-fed canal originating at the Tikabhairav intake in the forested hills south of Lalitpur and terminating, in its historical form, at Saptapatal Pokhari in Lagankhel. It is one of the principal hydraulic alignments of the Kathmandu Valley and, on the basis of epigraphic evidence summarised in Chapter 2, may represent a substantially older hydraulic tradition than its Licchavi-period documentation alone would suggest.

This report presents the findings of a GPS-based field survey conducted in May 2022 covering 94 canal sections and 68 georeferenced points of interest, together with a desktop hydraulic analysis grounded in the elevation benchmarks of the Department of Irrigation's 2020 Rajkulo study.

**The central finding is that the canal's deterioration is anthropogenic, not hydraulic.** A bed slope of **S = 0.01529** (1 in 65), computed from the GPS-measured length of 10,668.5 m and the 163.14 m total elevation drop documented by the Department of Irrigation's 2020 benchmarks, is approximately **35 times steeper than Lacey's regime slope** for the canal's plausible design discharge. The slope estimate is robust to the alternative length convention used in earlier drafts (the AutoCAD-refined main-alignment length of 13,158.2 m yields S = 0.01240, or roughly 28 times Lacey's regime slope; see Chapter 6 for both). Manning's equation applied across the lining materials documented in the field — concrete, brick masonry, stone masonry, earth, and vegetated earth — yields flow velocities that comfortably exceed Kennedy's critical velocity for the cross-sectional depths observed, by factors of 2.6 to 9.7 across every lining type. Under free-flowing, unobstructed conditions the canal possesses ample hydraulic energy to maintain self-cleansing flow. Restoration does not require redesigning the canal's slope or geometry; it requires removing obstructions, rehabilitating the intake, intercepting sewage, and clearing silt.

The condition data, however, document a system in advanced disrepair. Perennial flow persists for only **3,432.6 m (27.9 % of the alignment)**, terminating abruptly at Chainage 3+124 m. Beyond this point the canal is seasonal, dry, or carries sewage. **Silting is confirmed in 43.3 % of the surveyed length**, with the heaviest accumulation at the intake itself, where deposits of 1.5–2.0 m and a non-functional sluice gate have reduced design discharge to a small fraction of capacity. **Combined sewage, storm-water, and waste-dumping contamination affects 21.4 % of the directly observable alignment**, concentrated in the 5–7 km band and at the Lagankhel approach. **3,182.9 m (24.4 %) of the canal is buried beneath roads and urban infrastructure** at four confirmed segments, representing the principal structural obstacle to full restoration.

A quantitative restoration priority matrix — scoring each one-kilometre band on contamination, silting, and encroachment severity — identifies the **5–7 km band as the most critical zone for intervention**, with the 3–5 km, 8–9 km, and 10–11 km bands ranked high priority. The upper reach (0–3 km), although hydraulically intact, requires intake rehabilitation and an early sewage interception at Chainage 0+524.5 m to protect the canal's only remaining clean water source.

**Restoration as a functioning feeder for Saptapatal Pokhari is assessed as technically feasible**, contingent on four conditions: rehabilitation of the intake structure and sluice gate; sewage interception at the six identified inflow points; resolution of road-over-canal encroachments through covered-channel construction, horizontal directional drilling, or rerouting; and construction of a small-scale water-quality treatment facility immediately upstream of the pond. **None of these obstacles are hydraulic. All are institutional, financial, and political.**

This report does not attempt to specify the detailed engineering design of those interventions. It instead provides the empirical baseline — alignment geometry, condition catalogue, water quality regime, hydraulic adequacy assessment, environmental and public health risk framing, restoration priority matrix, stakeholder framework, and phased-intervention plan — required before such a design can responsibly proceed. The single most important technical follow-up identified by this study is a total-station survey to determine per-section slope; the single most important institutional follow-up is the establishment of a legal protection corridor for the canal alignment across both Godavari Municipality and Lalitpur Metropolitan City jurisdictions.

The path forward is well defined. What is now required is the institutional will to follow it.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background — hydraulic heritage of the Kathmandu Valley

Water management has been central to settled life in the Kathmandu Valley since at least the early historic period. The Valley's three medieval cities — Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur — developed in close hydrological relationship with the surrounding upland watersheds, sustained by an integrated infrastructure of canals, ponds, fields, and below-ground stone-spout systems that operated together as a single hydrologic mechanism. Surface water from hill springs and rain-fed catchments was conveyed through hill-fed canals (*rajkulo*) to irrigate the surrounding farmland and to recharge the urban ponds (*pokhari*); infiltration from canals and ponds replenished the shallow aquifer; that aquifer in turn discharged at controlled points through the stone-spout (*hiti*) network that supplied drinking water to the historic cores. The fact that this was a coherent system — not a collection of independent features — has been documented at length by Padma Sundar Joshi's *Hiti Pranali* and is a precondition for understanding the present study.

The rajkulo–pokhari–hiti system has, in the last fifty years, experienced near-systemic disruption. Rapid urbanisation across the southern Valley has converted irrigation command areas to housing plots, narrowed canal corridors, buried canal alignments beneath roads, and connected domestic and storm drainage directly to surviving canal channels. The result is a Valley in which the historic ponds are drying, the historic hiti are running thin or dry, and the canal alignments that once knit the system together are silted, contaminated, or invisible. The loss is hydrological, ecological, and cultural at once.

### 1.2 The Tikabhairab Rajkulo

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo is one of the principal canals of this system. It originates at the Tikabhairav intake (approximately 27.574°N, 85.318°E; 1,477.16 m ASL) in the forested hills south of Lalitpur and runs, in its historical configuration, approximately 13 km north through the settlements of Chapagaon, Pyangaon, Thecho, Sunakothi, and Dholahiti to terminate at Saptapatal Pokhari in Lagankhel (1,314.02 m ASL). The total elevation drop of 163.14 m provides a hydraulically energetic alignment capable, under unobstructed conditions, of self-cleansing flow throughout its length (the technical demonstration is the subject of Chapter 6).

The canal is documented in Licchavi-period inscriptions and was most recently formally restored, in part, in 2019 BS (approximately 1962 CE). It served three interconnected functions historically: irrigation of the southern Lalitpur hinterland; recharge of Saptapatal Pokhari at the Lagankhel terminus; and, through Saptapatal Pokhari and aquifer pathways, indirect support of the hiti network in Lalitpur's historic core. As of the May 2022 field survey, none of these functions is operating at scale.

### 1.3 Study rationale

Saptapatal Pokhari at Lagankhel — the canal's historical terminus — has experienced sharp declines in water level over the past two decades, attributable both to reduced surface-water inflow (the canal itself) and to falling groundwater levels in the surrounding aquifer. Restoring Tikabhairab Rajkulo as a feeder to the pokhari has been a recurring proposal in municipal and donor discussions but has not, to date, been preceded by the kind of systematic baseline assessment that would allow the engineering, governance, and environmental dimensions of restoration to be reasoned about in concert. This report is intended to fill that gap.

The May 2022 field survey was designed to produce a single, internally consistent baseline dataset documenting the canal's full surveyed alignment at the level of detail required for restoration planning: section-by-section condition, water quality, lining material, geometry, and flow regime, all with GPS-referenced positioning. The accompanying desktop analyses — hydraulic, risk-based, and priority-scored — convert that dataset into the four planning artefacts (hydraulic adequacy assessment, environmental risk framing, restoration priority matrix, phased intervention plan) that the report's recommended next steps depend on.

### 1.4 Objectives

The study has six objectives:

1. Document the physical condition, geometry, lining material, and water quality of the full surveyed canal alignment from the Tikabhairav intake to the Rajdal Barrack / Lagankhel terminus.
2. Identify the canal's flow regime (perennial, seasonal, interrupted, disturbed) and map the spatial distribution of silting, contamination, and encroachment.
3. Conduct hydraulic analysis using bed-slope calculation, Lacey's regime slope, Kennedy's critical velocity formula, and Manning's equation to evaluate whether the canal's original design is adequate for restoration.
4. Construct a quantitative restoration priority matrix scoring each 1 km band of the alignment against contamination, silting, and encroachment severity.
5. Assess the engineering, governance, and maintenance challenges associated with restoring the canal as a feeder for Saptapatal Pokhari, and the environmental and public health risks of doing so without parallel sewage management.
6. Document the canal's heritage significance, including the epigraphic evidence whose interpretation is summarised in §2.3, and identify research priorities for future study.

### 1.5 Scope and study area

The study covers the main alignment of Tikabhairab Rajkulo from intake to terminus, together with two branch loops and two open-ended branches surveyed for evaluation as alternative routes. Administratively, the alignment crosses two jurisdictions — Godavari Municipality in the upper and middle reaches and Lalitpur Metropolitan City in the lower urban reaches — a fact whose implications for restoration governance are taken up in Chapter 9.

The study explicitly *excludes* the following, which are identified as priorities for follow-up work:

- Per-section bed-slope measurement requiring total-station survey (discussed in §6.8 and §12.1);
- Laboratory quantification of water quality at the six identified sewage inflow points and at the terminus (§12.3);
- Command-area mapping of the agricultural parcels historically served by the canal (§12.4);
- Continuous hydrological monitoring of intake discharge across seasons (§12.5);
- Detailed household-level social survey of communities along the alignment (§12.6).

The Tikabhairav intake structure itself is included only at the level of visual inspection; a full structural assessment of the intake works is identified as a Phase 1 engineering deliverable in Chapter 10, not as part of this baseline survey.

### 1.6 Report structure

The report is organised into thirteen chapters and six appendices. Chapters 1–2 establish background and heritage context. Chapter 3 documents methodology. Chapters 4–5 present the field-condition findings (structure, alignment, flow, water quality). Chapter 6 presents the hydraulic analysis and establishes the central technical finding that the canal's design is sound. Chapter 7 presents the new environmental and public health risk framework. Chapter 8 presents the quantitative restoration priority matrix. Chapter 9 presents the stakeholder and governance framework. Chapter 10 presents the phased intervention plan. Chapters 11–12 catalogue residual challenges and research gaps. Chapter 13 concludes.

## 2. Heritage Significance and Historical Context

### 2.1 The integrated hydraulic system of the Kathmandu Valley

The rajkulo, the pokhari, and the hiti form three components of a single hydrological apparatus that has historically defined the spatial and ritual organisation of the Kathmandu Valley's urban settlements. The components were not independently designed; their geometric, hydraulic, and elevational relationships were calibrated to one another and to the field systems and groundwater regime in which they were embedded.

A rajkulo's alignment was determined by two competing constraints: the elevation required to deliver flow under gravity to its command area and to its terminal pokhari; and the topographic feasibility of the alignment between intake and terminus. The rajkulo intake itself was located where surface flow could be reliably abstracted year-round — typically a spring-fed or low-order stream at sufficient elevation that command-area delivery was possible by gravity alone. Tikabhairav meets both criteria: a spring-fed source at 1,477.16 m ASL, situated such that the 163.14 m elevation differential to Saptapatal Pokhari (1,314.02 m ASL) is hydraulically generous.

The pokhari at the canal's terminus served a dual function. It buffered the canal's seasonal variability — storing water during high-flow periods for use during low-flow ones — and it acted as a recharge reservoir for the local aquifer through bed infiltration. The downstream hiti network in the historic core drew on that aquifer through a system of bevelled spout outlets at controlled depths.

The system is therefore tightly coupled. A failing canal does not simply lose its irrigation function; it also drains the pokhari that it fed and, in time, the aquifer that the pokhari recharged, and ultimately the hiti that the aquifer fed. The reverse is equally true: restoring the canal restores the pond, which restores aquifer recharge, which restores the hiti. The system can fail or recover as a unit. The case for restoring Tikabhairab Rajkulo accordingly cannot be made purely on irrigation grounds; it must be made — and is made in this report — on integrated hydrological-cultural grounds.

### 2.2 The Licchavi hydraulic record

The Licchavi dynasty (c. 4th–9th century CE) provides the earliest substantial epigraphic record of formal canal administration in the Kathmandu Valley. Licchavi inscriptions, surviving on stone pillars and copper plates at numerous Valley locations, document royal patronage of canal construction and maintenance; the imposition of canal-related taxes; the regulation of water distribution among command-area villages; and the appointment of officials responsible for canal upkeep. Several inscriptions explicitly reference the canal known today as Tikabhairab Rajkulo, attesting to its integration into Licchavi-period agricultural administration.

The administrative and religious language of the Licchavi state was Sanskrit. Inscriptions, royal orders, land grants, and infrastructure designations of the period are recorded in Sanskrit, and the technical nomenclature applied to formally state-built infrastructure under Licchavi patronage is, with rare exceptions, Sanskritic in origin. This is consistent with the broader pattern of administrative-language adoption documented across South Asian early-medieval polities.

### 2.3 The Tilmak question

Against this background, the Licchavi-period name for the canal that this report concerns presents an interpretive problem worth taking seriously. As documented in Padma Sundar Joshi's *Hiti Pranali*, Licchavi inscriptions refer to the canal by the term **'Tilmak'** *[CITATION: Hiti Pranali, page TK — to be inserted once the specific passage is located]* — a word with no traceable Sanskrit root.

A Sanskrit-administering dynasty whose other infrastructure designations are Sanskritic would be expected, on the prior pattern, to assign a Sanskritic name to a state canal. The persistence of a non-Sanskritic name in formal royal records is therefore anomalous and invites explanation. At least three explanations can be considered, each with different implications for the canal's heritage classification.

**Explanation 1: Inheritance of name (and probably alignment) from the pre-Licchavi Kirat period.** Under this reading, the canal — or at least its established course and local designation — predates the arrival of Licchavi administration and was inherited from the Kirat communities who were the attested pre-Licchavi inhabitants of the Valley. Licchavi inscriptions would then record an existing toponym rather than bestow a new one. This explanation is consistent with the general pattern observed elsewhere in South Asia, in which incoming administrations have selectively absorbed and institutionalised the hydraulic practices of predecessor communities.

**Explanation 2: Borrowing from a local non-Sanskrit hydraulic lexicon contemporaneous with the Licchavi period.** Under this reading, 'Tilmak' was a vernacular term in use among local communities during the Licchavi period without itself implying pre-Licchavi origins. The inscription would then reflect Licchavi adoption of vernacular usage for what may have been a Licchavi-era construction.

**Explanation 3: Scribal preservation of a vernacular designation despite Sanskritic administrative practice.** Under this reading, 'Tilmak' could have entered formal records as a fixed proper noun whose original meaning was no longer transparent at the time of inscription, with no implication about either pre-Licchavi origin or the timing of construction.

These three explanations are not exhaustive and are not mutually exclusive; the first and the second, for example, are compatible if a Kirat-period name was retained as vernacular usage into the Licchavi period. The point of distinguishing them is that the three carry different evidentiary burdens and different conservation implications. Explanation 1, if confirmed, would place Tikabhairab Rajkulo among the oldest continuously utilised irrigation alignments documented in South Asia, with substantial implications for international heritage classification. Explanations 2 and 3 leave the canal's significance considerable but bounded by its Licchavi-period documentation.

The hypothesis cannot be resolved by the field survey reported here. Resolution requires three lines of work, identified in §12.2 as priority follow-up research: (i) systematic epigraphic review of all Licchavi inscriptions referencing 'Tilmak', with attention to the contexts in which the term appears (irrigation orders, land grants, religious endowments) and any internal evidence about how the term was understood at the time of inscription; (ii) archaeological investigation of the subsoil stratigraphy at and immediately downstream of the Tikabhairav intake structure, capable of dating the earliest extant canal works; and (iii) comparative toponymic analysis of other non-Sanskritic hydraulic names in Licchavi-period records, to establish whether 'Tilmak' is anomalous or part of a recognisable pattern.

### 2.4 Heritage classification implications

The case for treating Tikabhairab Rajkulo as a heritage asset under Nepal's monument-protection framework does not depend on resolution of the Tilmak question. Its documented Licchavi-period status, the 2019 BS / 1962 CE intake restoration, and its role in a continuously functioning Valley hydraulic system already establish the case for formal protection of the canal corridor under national heritage legislation. Resolution of the Tilmak question would strengthen the case for international recognition and may, depending on the outcome, expand the funding sources available for restoration. The present report does not assume any particular outcome on this question but treats the question as deserving the resources to answer.

The practical consequence — taken up in Chapter 9 — is that any restoration framework should treat the Tikabhairab Rajkulo corridor as a heritage asset deserving the kind of legal protection that prevents continued encroachment, regardless of how the Tilmak question is ultimately resolved.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Research design

The study combines two methods: a structured field survey of the canal's full alignment, conducted in May 2022, and a desktop analysis of the field-collected data including hydraulic computation, priority scoring, and risk framing. The two methods are not sequential but iterative — early hydraulic analysis informed mid-survey refinements to data capture, and final-stage analyses (priority matrix, risk framework) drew on both field observation and structured re-examination of the survey dataset.

The field survey adopts a section-based unit of analysis. Each surveyed section is a contiguous segment of canal with uniform attributes (lining material, geometry, flow regime, water quality category, silting status). Section boundaries are drawn where any of these attributes change. The result is 94 surveyed sections of variable length covering the GPS-measured alignment from intake to Lagankhel / Naricha terminus, together with two surveyed branch loops and two open-ended branches.

A parallel record of points of interest (POIs) was maintained for discrete features not amenable to section-based representation: cross-drainage structures, sewage inflow points, landslide-vulnerable locations, road crossings, hume-pipe transitions, and encroachment sites. A total of 68 POIs were recorded with GPS coordinates, photographs, and supplementary observations.

### 3.2 Field survey protocol

A structured data-collection flowchart guided surveyors from initial section identification to attribute classification. The flowchart sequence: stretch code and street name recording → in-situ visibility check → lined / unlined status and material identification → geometric section classification (rectangular, hume pipe / circular) → flow regime classification (perennial, seasonal, interrupted, unknown) → silting status → secondary canal presence → water quality classification (river water, storm water, sewage, waste dumping; multi-select) → photographic documentation.

Canal dimensions (width, depth, lining thickness where measurable) were measured using a steel tape. Where vegetation or silt accumulation prevented direct measurement, dimensions were estimated from visible structural elements and flagged in field notes; in heavily silted sections, only top width and overall structural form could be reliably recorded. Manual data collection via pen-and-paper was maintained alongside digital entry on the GPS-based field application for verification and redundancy.

Water quality classification was qualitative and observational. The four categories (river water, storm water, sewage, waste dumping) were recorded as multi-select values per section because contamination in the field was frequently multi-source — a section might carry simultaneous sewage, storm-water, and solid-waste inputs from adjacent informal urban drainage. No laboratory sampling was conducted in this survey phase; quantitative water-quality characterisation is identified as a follow-up requirement in §12.3.

### 3.3 Spatial data acquisition

Canal alignment geometry was recorded using a GPS-based mobile field-data-collection application (Mergin Maps / Input App) running on a smartphone, with a Google Satellite imagery layer for in-field reference. The canal was tracked as a series of GPS line features, one per surveyed section. Where a dedicated GPS device was available, its readings were preferred over smartphone GPS at section boundary points to reduce horizontal positional error.

The horizontal accuracy of consumer-grade GPS receivers under partial canopy cover is typically ±3–10 m, which is adequate for the alignment-mapping purposes of this study. The vertical accuracy of the same receivers is substantially worse — typically ±10–30 m — and is **not** sufficient for per-section bed-slope calculation. GPS-derived elevation data is accordingly not used in the hydraulic analysis of this report. Elevation is anchored instead on the two fixed benchmarks documented in the Department of Irrigation's 2020 Rajkulo study (intake 1,477.16 m ASL; terminus 1,314.02 m ASL).

### 3.4 Length measurement and reconciliation

Three different length figures appear in this report and must be distinguished:

- **GPS-measured length: 10,668.5 m.** The sum of GPS line geometries across the 94 surveyed sections. Represents the canal length physically walked and tracked. Where sections were buried beneath roads and not directly walkable, no GPS data was recorded.
- **AutoCAD-refined length (main alignment): 13,158.2 m.** The total inferred length of the main alignment from intake to terminus, including buried sections estimated from satellite imagery and from continuity inference between adjacent visible canal segments. Represents the best available estimate of the full canal length under current conditions.
- **Terminus chainage: 11,773.3 m (Lagankhel) / 11,590.6 m (Naricha).** The chainage at the canal's terminal points. The difference between the terminus chainage and the AutoCAD-refined total (~1,385 m) corresponds to the length of branch loops and open-ended branches also surveyed.

The implications of the GPS-vs-AutoCAD difference for slope computation are discussed in §6.1; the slope at the GPS-measured length is adopted as primary, with the alternative shown for sensitivity.

### 3.5 Hydraulic analysis framework

The hydraulic analysis adopts four standard tools of open-channel hydraulics in sequence:

1. **Bed-slope calculation** from fixed elevation benchmarks and channel length, producing the average gradient over the surveyed alignment.
2. **Kennedy's critical velocity** (Vc = 0.55 · m · D⁰·⁶⁴) to establish the silt-transport threshold for the observed hydraulic depths and standard South Asian alluvial silt grade.
3. **Lacey's regime slope** (S₀ = f⁵ᐟ³ / (3340 · Q¹ᐟ⁶) in metric) to compare the canal's actual gradient against the equilibrium gradient that an alluvial channel would self-form for its design discharge.
4. **Manning's equation** (V = (1/n) · R²ᐟ³ · S¹ᐟ²) to compute mean flow velocity under each lining type observed in the field.

All four computations require, implicitly or explicitly, a reference discharge Q. No flow measurement was conducted during the May 2022 survey: the intake was heavily silted and the sluice gate non-functional, so any spot measurement would have characterised the disabled rather than the design discharge. A reference discharge of **Q = 0.10 m³/s (100 L/s)** is adopted parametrically. The qualitative conclusions of Chapter 6 are robust to plausible variation in Q across the 0.05–0.50 m³/s range.

Site-specific values of Kennedy's critical-velocity ratio m and Lacey's silt factor f are unknown in the absence of sediment-grade analysis. Conservative defaults (m = 1.0, f = 1.0) are used. Refinement of these parameters through sediment sampling is identified as a follow-up activity in §6.8 but is not expected to alter the qualitative findings of the chapter.

### 3.6 Restoration priority scoring methodology

The restoration priority matrix in Chapter 8 scores each one-kilometre band of the alignment on three parameters, each weighted equally:

- **Contamination score (0–4).** Composite assessment of water quality across the band: 0 = clean river water only; 1 = storm water only; 2 = sewage present; 4 = combined sewage and waste dumping. The 0–4 scale is non-linear because the absolute severity of combined contamination exceeds, by ecological and remediation-cost measures, the marginal cost of single-source contamination.
- **Silting score (0–2).** 0 = silting absent; 1 = uncertain or band is largely underground and not directly assessable; 2 = silting confirmed present across the band.
- **Encroachment score (0–2).** 0 = no encroachment observed; 1 = partial or adjacent construction with potential future impact; 2 = road on canal or full structural obstruction confirmed.

Band scores were assigned by reviewing the GPS-referenced section attributes and POIs falling within each 1 km chainage band and aggregating to a band-level value. Where multiple sections within a band differed in attribute value, the area-weighted modal value was used (i.e. the value characterising the majority of the band's length).

Composite band scores are summed (maximum = 8) and classified into four priority tiers:
- **Critical** ≥ 5.0 — immediate restoration intervention required;
- **High** 3.5 to < 5.0 — intervention required in early restoration phases;
- **Medium** 2.0 to < 3.5 — intervention required but not immediately critical;
- **Low** < 2.0 — minor maintenance only.

The scoring framework is deliberately simple. Its limitations — equal weighting across three different qualitative axes; coarse 1 km resolution; subjective elements in the assignment of band-level values — are discussed in §8.4. The framework's purpose is to support sequencing decisions in restoration planning, not to replace detailed engineering assessment of individual reaches.

### 3.8 Survey limitations

The following limitations are identified and should be borne in mind when interpreting the report's findings:

1. **No total-station survey.** Per-section bed slope cannot be determined from GPS altitude (±10–30 m vertical error) or from linear interpolation between two fixed benchmarks. Only the average bed slope is reported. Per-section slope refinement is identified as the priority follow-up in §12.1.
2. **Underground / inaccessible sections.** 3,182.9 m of the AutoCAD-refined alignment (24.4 %) is buried beneath roads or otherwise not directly observable. Attributes for these sections are inferred from satellite imagery and from adjacent visible sections; direct verification was not possible.
3. **Heavily silted sections.** In sections where silt deposits and overgrown vegetation prevented physical measurement, dimensions were estimated or omitted; the section attributes for these reaches are less reliable than for clean reaches.
4. **Inconsistent original lining material.** Partial reconstruction of the canal over different historical periods means that the original construction material is not consistently identifiable along the alignment; current material (as observed in 2022) is recorded but does not always correspond to original.
5. **One-sided lining variation.** Several sections are lined on one side (typically against a natural rock face) and unlined on the other, with dimensions varying along the same section. Where this is the case, sectional dimensions reflect the dominant configuration but may not characterise the section's hydraulic performance fully.
6. **Qualitative water quality.** No laboratory sampling. Water quality classification is observational and represents conditions at the time of survey (May 2022; pre-monsoon).
7. **Origin period not determinable.** The canal's earliest construction period cannot be inferred from this survey; epigraphic and archaeological investigation is required (§12.2).

Despite these limitations, the dataset collected is the most systematic baseline survey of the canal completed to date and is sufficient for the planning purposes for which this report is intended.

## 4. Canal Structure and Alignment

### 4.1 Alignment and settlements traversed

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo originates at the Tikabhairav intake structure (Chainage 0+000 m, 1,477.16 m ASL) and runs north through a sequence of villages and peri-urban settlements before reaching the Lagankhel terminus (Chainage 11+773.3 m, 1,314.02 m ASL). ### 4.3 Lining materials and construction

Lining is present in **7,574.5 m (71.0 %)** of the GPS-measured length. Lining material varies along the alignment:

- The upper reaches, where the canal runs along natural rock formations (from approximately Chainage 1+154 m onward), are characteristically one-sidedly lined with stone masonry against the rock face; the opposite bank is unlined or earthen. This is consistent with an original construction philosophy that exploited natural rock faces as one canal wall — economical and durable, and characteristic of the Valley's traditional hydraulic practice.
- Mid-alignment sections show mixed lining: stone masonry in older reaches, brick masonry in repaired reaches, and concrete in modern interventions.
- Lower urban sections are typically concrete-lined or, where road encroachment has occurred, fully enclosed in concrete or hume-pipe culverts.

Periodic annual cleaning is undertaken by local communities before the monsoon. The removed silt is, however, typically piled along the canal banks rather than being transported away — a practice that, while well-intentioned, gives several sections the misleading appearance from above of an unlined or collapsed canal even where the lining is intact beneath the silt pile.

### 4.4 Intake structure at Tikabhairav

The intake structure at Tikabhairav, formally restored in 2019 BS (approximately 1962 CE), is heavily silted. The weir is filled with sediment to a depth restricting passive flow; the sluice gate that historically controlled discharge into the canal is non-functional and cannot regulate flow even when intake water is present. Silt accumulation in the immediately downstream channel reaches 1.5–2.0 m in depth, decreasing gradually downstream as residual flow velocity provides some sediment-transport capacity.

The combination of weir siltation, non-functional sluice gate, and accumulated near-intake silt has reduced the canal's effective discharge to a small fraction of its design capacity. This is the binding constraint on the entire downstream system (see §6.7) and the primary focus of Phase 1 restoration interventions (§10.2).

- **AutoCAD length** = inferred total alignment including buried sections;
- **Terminus chainage** = cumulative chainage along the main alignment only, excluding branch loops.

The difference between the AutoCAD total (13,158.2 m) and the terminus chainage (11,773.3 m) — approximately 1,385 m — corresponds to the surveyed branch loops and open-ended branches, which are part of the alignment dataset but not on the intake-to-terminus main route.

### 5.1 Flow regime distribution

The proportion of canal length under each flow condition, as determined from the AutoCAD-refined alignment, is:

| Flow condition | Length (m) | % of total | Primary chainage range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perennial | 3,432.6 | 27.9 % | 0+000 to 3+124 m |

| Disturbed (sewage / storm-flow dominant) | 2,453.6 | 20.0 % | Multiple sewage / storm inflow zones |

***Table 5.1 — Flow regime distribution along the main canal alignment. Categories overlap where 'seasonal' reaches also experience 'disturbed' sewage inflows; totals exceed 100 % accordingly.***

Under current intake and encroachment conditions, even if the canal were entirely cleared of silt, only 27.9 % of its alignment would carry usable water year-round. Restoring perennial useful flow across the full alignment requires both intake rehabilitation (to restore design discharge) and sewage diversion (to remove contaminated inflows that currently dominate the lower-alignment flow).

### 5.2 The perennial-to-sewage transition zone

The transition from clean perennial flow to seasonal-and-contaminated flow is spatially abrupt. From the intake to **Chainage 3+124 m**, the canal carries perennial flow sustained by the Tikabhairav spring and subsurface seepage. At **Chainage 3+241.8 m** — approximately 118 m downstream — the canal transitions to stagnant water with no observable dry-season discharge. From **Chainage 3+253.8 m** onward, the only significant inflows are sewage and storm-water runoff from adjacent urban development. By **Chainage 3+976.4 m**, the canal was observed in May 2022 to be completely dry.

The 130 m transition zone between Chainage 3+124 m and 3+253.8 m is therefore a spatially precise boundary marking the limit of the rehabilitable upper canal under current conditions and the starting point of the lower-alignment remediation challenge. It is the most important spatial feature for restoration planning and is the natural upper boundary of the Phase 2 sewage-interception scope (§10.3).

### 5.5 Implications for restoration water quality

The flow and water quality findings have three direct implications for restoration planning:

1. **Sewage interception is non-optional.** Restoring flow without addressing the six identified inflows would, by simple mass-balance, transfer concentrated sewage into Saptapatal Pokhari at the terminus. The pond's cultural significance, ecological function, and shallow groundwater recharge role would all be compromised — converting a restoration project into a pollution vector. This concern is taken up quantitatively in §7.2.
2. **The clean / contaminated boundary is geographically precise.** The 130 m transition zone at Chainage 3+124–3+253.8 m means that Phase 1 restoration (intake + upper canal) can deliver clean water into the upper reach without immediate concern about contamination from the lower alignment. Phase 1 is therefore a hydraulically and ecologically meaningful intervention on its own merits, not contingent on completion of Phase 2.
3. **Below Chainage 9+665 m, water quality cannot be directly observed.** The road-buried lower alignment (Road-4 in Table 4.3) precludes verification of flow content until either the road-over-canal section is reconstructed (a Phase 3 intervention) or sample-point access is restored. Phase 3 should accordingly include provision for water-quality monitoring access at multiple chainages.

## 6. Hydraulic Analysis

This chapter evaluates whether the Tikabhairab Rajkulo's original hydraulic design is adequate to support restoration, or whether the design itself contributes to the deterioration observed in the field survey. The analysis is built from four standard tools of open-channel hydraulics applied in sequence: bed-slope calculation from elevation benchmarks; Kennedy's critical velocity to establish the silt-transport threshold; Lacey's regime slope to compare actual against ideal alluvial-channel gradient; and Manning's equation to compute the flow velocity achievable under each lining type observed in the alignment.

The analysis is desktop in nature. It is anchored on the two fixed elevation reference points documented in the Department of Irrigation's 2020 Rajkulo study — 1,477.16 m ASL at the Tikabhairav intake and 1,314.02 m ASL at the Lagankhel terminus — and on the canal length recovered from this study's GPS survey and AutoCAD refinement. It does **not** rest on per-section elevation data, which consumer-grade GPS receivers cannot supply at the vertical accuracy required (typical error ±10–30 m). The limitations this imposes are addressed explicitly in §6.8.

### 6.1 Elevation profile and bed slope

The total elevation drop along the main alignment is

$$\Delta H = 1{,}477.16 - 1{,}314.02 = 163.14 \text{ m}$$

This survey records two distinct length figures for the same alignment, which must be reconciled before slope can be computed:

- **GPS-measured length** (sum of GPS line geometries across the 94 surveyed sections): **10,668.5 m**.
- **AutoCAD-refined length** of the main alignment, including underground and inaccessible sections inferred from satellite imagery: **13,158.2 m**.

The bed slope, computed against both length values:

| Length basis | L (m) | S | Equivalent | Angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPS-measured (surveyed sections only) | 10,668.5 | **0.01529** | **1 in 65** | **0.876°** |
| AutoCAD-refined (full main alignment) | 13,158.2 | 0.01240 | 1 in 81 | 0.710° |

***Table 6.1 — Bed slope under two length conventions.***

**The GPS-measured slope, S = 0.01529 (1 in 65), is adopted as the primary value for the hydraulic computations that follow.** It is anchored on the canal length physically walked and GPS-recorded across the 94 surveyed sections — directly observed data rather than inferred path. The AutoCAD-refined slope (S = 0.01240) is shown as an alternative for sensitivity: it uses an inferred full alignment length that includes buried sections estimated from satellite imagery, and its accuracy is contingent on the accuracy of those inferences. Both are presented because the choice affects downstream numerical values by approximately 10 % (slope scales linearly; velocity scales as S¹ᐟ², so velocity changes by ≈ 5 %).

The qualitative conclusions of this chapter — and of the report — do not depend on which slope value is selected. Where the difference matters numerically, sensitivity values at the AutoCAD-refined slope are noted in line. Resolution of the choice requires a total-station survey from intake to terminus, identified in §12.1 as the priority follow-up. The earlier informal trigonometric estimate of 1.55° (≈ S = 0.027) used in pre-publication drafts is incorrect and is not used in this report.

The slope so computed is in any case an **average** across the full alignment. Per-section slope varies considerably — some segments are locally steep while others, particularly in the urban lower alignment, are nearly flat where the channel has been partially infilled. Determining the per-section slope distribution requires a total-station survey, identified as the priority follow-up in §12.1.

### 6.2 Reference discharge for hydraulic computation

Kennedy's, Lacey's, and Manning's equations all require, implicitly or explicitly, a reference discharge Q. No flow measurement was conducted during the May 2022 survey; the intake was heavily silted and the sluice gate non-functional, so any spot measurement would have characterised the disabled rather than the design discharge.

For the hydraulic analyses that follow, a **design reference discharge of Q = 0.10 m³/s (100 L/s)** is adopted. This value is consistent with the canal's documented historical role as both an irrigation channel for the southern Lalitpur command area and a feeder for Saptapatal Pokhari. The assumption is parametric: results scale predictably with Q (regime slope as Q⁻¹ᐟ⁶; Kennedy's Vc independent of Q; Manning's V independent of Q at fixed section). The qualitative conclusions of this chapter are robust to a Q-range of approximately 0.05–0.50 m³/s.

A direct measurement of the canal's natural-spring base discharge at the rehabilitated intake is identified as a research priority (§12.5).

### 6.3 Kennedy's critical velocity

Kennedy's critical velocity Vc is the minimum mean cross-sectional velocity at which sediment of a given grade is maintained in suspension and transported through the channel without depositing. Below Vc the channel silts; above Vc, sediment is transported and the channel self-cleanses. For the alluvial conditions characteristic of South Asian irrigation canals, Kennedy proposed

$$V_c = 0.55 \times m \times D^{0.64}$$

where D is the hydraulic mean depth (cross-sectional flow area divided by top width, in metres) and m is a critical-velocity ratio dependent on silt grade (m = 1.0 for standard alluvial silt, the value adopted here in the absence of site-specific sediment characterisation).

Kennedy's critical velocity for representative hydraulic depths spanning the range observed in this survey:

| Hydraulic depth D (m) | Critical velocity Vc (m/s) | Representative section type |
|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.255 | Shallow / silted reach |
| 0.40 | 0.306 | Typical small section |
| 0.50 | 0.354 | Representative main section |
| 0.60 | 0.397 | Deeper / larger section |

***Table 6.2 — Kennedy's critical velocity at observed hydraulic depths.***

These values establish the velocity threshold that the canal's flow must exceed under each combination of slope, lining, and discharge to remain self-cleansing.

### 6.4 Lacey's regime slope

Lacey's regime theory specifies the slope at which an alluvial channel, allowed to self-form, will neither aggrade nor degrade — the natural equilibrium gradient for a given discharge and sediment grade. For metric units:

$$S_0 = \frac{f^{5/3}}{3340 \cdot Q^{1/6}}$$

where f is Lacey's silt factor (f = 1.0 for standard alluvium; the field-observed coarse-gravel-and-silt mix is consistent with this value as a first approximation) and Q is the design discharge in m³/s.

For the adopted reference values f = 1.0 and Q = 0.10 m³/s:

$$S_0 = \frac{1.0^{5/3}}{3340 \cdot (0.10)^{1/6}} = \frac{1}{3340 \times 0.681} = 0.000440$$

The primary bed slope (S = 0.01529, per §6.1) is therefore

$$\frac{S}{S_0} = \frac{0.01529}{0.000440} \approx 35$$

times Lacey's regime slope. *Sensitivity to length convention:* at the alternative AutoCAD-refined slope (S = 0.01240) the ratio falls to approximately 28. *Sensitivity to discharge:* at Q = 0.05 m³/s the ratio rises to approximately 39; at Q = 0.50 m³/s it falls to approximately 27. Under all plausible combinations of length and discharge, the canal's slope substantially exceeds Lacey's regime slope. This is a substantial excess of gradient relative to alluvial equilibrium — the channel has far more hydraulic energy than is required for stable silt-transport behaviour.

The practical interpretation: under unobstructed flow conditions the canal will not naturally silt. The silting documented in the field survey — particularly the 1.5–2.0 m deposit at the intake — is therefore a consequence of *reduced velocity* caused by intake blockage and downstream encroachment, not of *insufficient slope*.

### 6.5 Manning's flow velocity by lining material

Manning's equation governs the mean velocity of flow in an open channel under steady, uniform conditions:

$$V = \frac{1}{n} \cdot R^{2/3} \cdot S^{1/2}$$

where n is Manning's roughness coefficient (a function of lining material), R is the hydraulic radius (flow area divided by wetted perimeter, in metres), and S is the bed slope.

A representative cross-section is required to evaluate this equation. For the rectangular geometry that dominates the alignment (64.5 % of total length per the §4.2 geometry distribution), a representative section width of 0.50 m and flow depth of 0.40 m is adopted:

$$A = 0.50 \times 0.40 = 0.20 \text{ m}^2, \quad P = 0.50 + 2 \times 0.40 = 1.30 \text{ m}, \quad R = \frac{0.20}{1.30} = 0.154 \text{ m}$$

With R = 0.154 m and the primary slope S = 0.01529:

$$R^{2/3} \times S^{1/2} = 0.287 \times 0.1236 = 0.0355$$

Manning's velocities at this representative section across the range of lining materials observed in the alignment:

| Lining material | Manning's n (range) | Velocity V (m/s) at S = 0.01529 | V/Vc (D = 0.40 m) | Silting risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete (smooth) | 0.012 – 0.018 | **2.96 – 1.97** | 9.7× – 6.4× | Negligible |
| Brick masonry | 0.015 – 0.025 | **2.37 – 1.42** | 7.7× – 4.6× | Very low |
| Stone masonry | 0.020 – 0.030 | **1.77 – 1.18** | 5.8× – 3.9× | Low |
| Earth (unlined) | 0.025 – 0.035 | **1.42 – 1.01** | 4.6× – 3.3× | Low |
| Vegetated earth (poor) | 0.030 – 0.045 | **1.18 – 0.79** | 3.9× – 2.6× | Moderate |

***Table 6.3 — Manning's velocity by lining material at the primary slope S = 0.01529 and representative section (w = 0.50 m, d = 0.40 m). At the alternative AutoCAD-refined slope (S = 0.01240) each velocity value is approximately 10 % lower; all V/Vc ratios remain well above unity.***

Across all five lining categories present in the alignment — including the worst-case vegetated-earth condition — the computed mean velocity comfortably exceeds Kennedy's critical velocity for the corresponding hydraulic depth. The lowest computed velocity (0.79 m/s for vegetated earth at n = 0.045) is approximately 2.6 times the critical velocity at the same depth (0.306 m/s for D = 0.40 m). The highest (2.96 m/s, smooth concrete at n = 0.012) is approximately 9.7 times Vc. At the alternative AutoCAD-refined slope, the corresponding range is 0.71 m/s (2.3× Vc) to 2.66 m/s (8.7× Vc) — qualitatively identical.

The Manning's velocities in this table differ from those published in pre-publication v1 drafts of this report, which used a different rounding convention for R²ᐟ³. The qualitative finding is unaffected — velocities exceed Vc across all lining types — but the numerical values in this report supersede those in earlier drafts.

### 6.6 Effect of cross-sectional geometry

The cross-sectional shape of the canal influences the hydraulic radius R and therefore the velocity V. Two principal geometries are observed in the alignment:

- **Rectangular sections (64.5 % of total length).** For a given cross-sectional area, rectangular geometry produces a relatively high hydraulic radius — higher than equivalent trapezoidal or triangular sections of the same area at low flow depths. The dominance of rectangular geometry in Tikabhairab Rajkulo is therefore hydraulically advantageous: it maximises velocity per unit discharge and minimises silting tendency.
- **Circular hume-pipe sections (12.0 % of total length).** Hydraulic radius in a circular pipe varies non-linearly with flow depth. At full flow, R is maximised and velocity is high. At partial flow — the condition observed throughout the alignment under current reduced discharge — the wetted perimeter grows more rapidly than flow area, reducing R, reducing velocity, and creating local conditions favourable to silt deposition. Hume-pipe sections are therefore disproportionately vulnerable to silting under the canal's present low-discharge regime.

This finding is significant for restoration planning: it identifies hume-pipe sections, despite their structural advantage in erosion-prone reaches, as the segments most at risk of silting if the intake is not first rehabilitated to restore design discharge.

### 6.7 The central hydraulic finding

The bed slope, regime-slope comparison, critical velocity, and Manning's velocity calculations converge on a single conclusion:

> **The Tikabhairab Rajkulo's hydraulic design is sound.** A bed slope of S = 0.01529 (1 in 65) provides approximately 35 times Lacey's regime slope under the canal's plausible discharge range, and yields Manning's velocities that exceed Kennedy's critical velocity by factors of 2.6 to 9.7 across every lining material documented in the alignment. The conclusion holds under the alternative slope convention (S = 0.01240): the ratio falls to 28× Lacey and the V/Vc range to 2.3–8.7×, both still far above the silting threshold. The silting, contamination, and flow interruption observed in the field survey are consequences of intake blockage, sewage loading, road-over-canal encroachment, and reduced discharge — not of inadequate design. **The canal's deterioration is anthropogenic.**

This finding establishes the empirical basis for the restoration strategy developed in Chapter 10. Because the canal's slope and geometry do not require redesign, restoration is reduced to a sequence of solvable engineering, sewage-management, and institutional tasks within the existing canal footprint.

A summary of the four hydraulic factors and their relationships to the canal's restoration prospects:

| Factor | Effect on velocity | Tikabhairab Rajkulo condition | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slope S | V ∝ S¹ᐟ² | S = 0.01529 primary (0.01240 alternative); 35× Lacey regime (28× alternative) | Excess slope under either length convention; silting is not slope-driven |
| Lining roughness n | V ∝ 1/n | Majority brick / stone masonry, 71 % lined | V exceeds Vc by 2.6× – 9.7× for every observed lining |
| Cross-section geometry | Higher R → higher V | 64.5 % rectangular; hydraulically optimal | Geometry does not require redesign |

### 6.8 Caveats and validation requirements

This analysis is sufficient to establish that the canal's design does not require modification. It is not sufficient to specify the detailed restoration engineering. Three categories of refinement are required before final design proceeds:

1. **Per-section slope determination.** The average bed slope reported here masks substantial local variation. Sections with locally flat gradients may require minor profile correction or drop-structure insertion; locally steep sections may need energy dissipators. A total-station survey from intake to terminus is identified in §12.1 as the priority next technical step.
2. **Direct discharge measurement.** All velocity computations in this chapter are parametric in Q. Once the intake is partially rehabilitated, a stage–discharge rating at the intake and at the terminus will replace the Q = 0.10 m³/s assumption with measured values.
3. **Sediment characterisation.** Kennedy's m = 1.0 and Lacey's f = 1.0 are conservative defaults. A sediment-grade analysis of the deposited material in the intake silt body and in representative downstream sections will refine both parameters and may modify the regime-slope multiplier.

These refinements would tighten the analysis but are not expected to alter its central conclusion. The conservative parameters used here produce a finding (28–35 × Lacey, V ≫ Vc for every lining) with sufficient margin that plausible parameter refinement cannot overturn it.

## 7. Environmental and Public Health Risk Assessment

The hydraulic analysis in Chapter 6 establishes that the canal's design can support restoration. The condition data in Chapters 4–5 establish that restoration is not a matter of building new infrastructure but of repairing existing infrastructure. Both findings, however, presume that restoration is desirable. This chapter examines the environmental and public health consequences of restoration — and of non-restoration — and identifies the risk-management measures that must accompany any physical intervention.

The framing here is that restoration is itself a risk-creating activity, not solely a risk-reducing one. Restoring flow through a canal whose lower alignment receives six untreated sewage inflows would convert the canal from a degraded local environment into a high-velocity contamination vector terminating in a culturally significant pond. The case for restoration depends on the credibility of the parallel measures that prevent this outcome.

### 7.1 Risk framework

Four risk categories are considered:

- **Water quality risk:** transport of contaminants from sewage inflow points to downstream receptors, particularly Saptapatal Pokhari at the terminus.
- **Vector-borne disease risk:** stagnant water in seasonal and disturbed reaches providing breeding habitat for mosquito vectors (*Aedes*, *Culex*, *Anopheles*) at the urban interface.
- **Groundwater risk:** contaminant infiltration from the canal to the shallow aquifer that supplies the historic Lalitpur hiti network and adjacent shallow domestic wells.
- **Climate-resilience risk:** vulnerability of the restored canal to inter-annual variability in monsoon precipitation and spring-fed intake reliability under projected climate trajectories.

Each category is assessed for current condition (do-nothing baseline), and for two scenarios: restoration without parallel water-management measures, and restoration with parallel measures. The point of distinguishing the two restoration scenarios is to make explicit the dependence of restoration benefit on parallel investments.

### 7.2 Contamination risk to Saptapatal Pokhari

Saptapatal Pokhari at Lagankhel is a culturally significant urban pond whose water levels have declined sharply over the past two decades. Its restoration would be one of the principal benefits claimed for any Tikabhairab Rajkulo restoration project. Yet under current canal conditions, restoring flow to the pond without parallel sewage management would transfer the six identified sewage inflows — plus diffuse waste-dumping loads observed across the 5–7 km critical band — directly into the pond.

A first-order mass-balance framing makes the issue concrete. The canal's lower alignment carries combined sewage, storm water, and waste dumping for 2,282.0 m of its directly observable length. At even modest discharge values, the cumulative load delivered to the terminus over a single restoration day would exceed the pond's typical fortnightly residence-time turnover under historical recharge conditions. The pond would not act as a buffer; it would act as a sink.

The downstream consequences would include:

- **Ecological degradation of the pond.** Eutrophication from nutrient loading; loss of aquatic biota; algal bloom risk during low-flow periods.
- **Public health risk.** The pond is used for ritual immersion and for proximate domestic water uses; direct human contact with sewage-loaded water would create acute health risk in the surrounding ward.
- **Groundwater contamination cascade.** The pond functions as an aquifer recharge feature. Contaminated pond water would infiltrate to the shallow aquifer that feeds the Lalitpur hiti network — converting a restoration project for the rajkulo into a contamination pathway for the hiti.
- **Reputational and political risk.** Visible contamination of a heritage pond in a flagship restoration project would compromise public confidence in heritage hydraulic restoration efforts more broadly across the Valley.

**Implication for restoration design.** Sewage interception at all six identified inflow points, together with a small-scale water-quality treatment facility immediately upstream of the pond, is non-optional. Restoring flow without these measures is not a partial restoration; it is the substitution of one form of damage for another.

### 7.3 Vector-borne disease risk in stagnant sections

The canal's seasonal and disturbed reaches (~80 % of the alignment under current conditions, per §5.1) contain stagnant or near-stagnant water for extended periods, particularly in the post-monsoon and dry-season transition. Stagnant water in urban-interface settings supports breeding of *Aedes aegypti* (dengue / chikungunya vector), *Culex* species (West Nile / Japanese encephalitis vectors), and *Anopheles* species (malaria vectors in lower-altitude reaches).

Kathmandu Valley has experienced expanding dengue prevalence over the past decade, with documented seasonal outbreaks in Lalitpur. The canal's lower alignment runs through high-density urban wards whose populations are directly exposed to any vector population sustained by canal-borne breeding habitat.

The risk is bidirectional with respect to restoration:

- **Status quo (no restoration).** Stagnant pools in the disturbed reach provide ongoing breeding habitat. Vector density is sustained at a level set by the current ratio of pooled to flowing water across seasons.
- **Restoration without intake rehabilitation.** Worst case. Some pools are refreshed but others, where infill or partial sewage flow creates new low-velocity zones, become more rather than less hospitable to vector breeding.
- **Full restoration (intake rehabilitated; sewage intercepted; design discharge restored).** Best case. Continuous high-velocity flow prevents pool formation across the full alignment; vector habitat is eliminated.

**Implication for restoration design.** Vector-breeding risk reinforces the case for intake rehabilitation in Phase 1 (§10.2): restoring discharge to design level converts the canal from a vector-habitat zone to a vector-free flowing channel. Partial restoration that leaves the lower alignment in a half-flowing state could be counterproductive.

### 7.4 Groundwater recharge implications

The hydraulic system schematic of §2.1 establishes that the canal contributes, through Saptapatal Pokhari and through direct bed infiltration along its alignment, to the shallow aquifer that feeds the Lalitpur hiti network. The implications are twofold and operate in tension.

**Recharge benefit.** A restored canal carrying perennial design discharge would substantially increase shallow-aquifer recharge across the southern Lalitpur urban area, supporting both the historic hiti network and the shallow domestic wells that supplement piped water supply in the same neighbourhoods. The magnitude of this benefit is not quantified in the present study; it would depend on canal-bed permeability, water-table position, and the volume of leakage through unlined and partially lined sections. Quantification is identified as a follow-up activity in §12.5.

**Contamination cascade risk.** The same infiltration pathway that delivers recharge benefit also delivers contaminants. Sewage-loaded water infiltrating from canal sections in the urban reach would enter the same aquifer system that the hiti depends on. The contamination cascade is not a theoretical concern: shallow groundwater contamination from informal urban drainage is already documented in Lalitpur.

**Implication for restoration design.** Restoration of canal-aquifer connectivity should be deliberate and selective. The upper reach (0–3 km), with confirmed clean perennial water and natural infiltration through unlined sections, can be allowed to recharge the aquifer beneficially. The middle and lower reaches, until sewage interception is complete, should be lined to prevent infiltration of contaminated flow. This is a design constraint on the Phase 3 urban-reach reconstruction (§10.4): impermeable lining is the appropriate default until upstream water quality is confirmed.

### 7.5 Climate resilience and monsoon variability

The canal's discharge is fundamentally rainfall-dependent. The Tikabhairav intake is fed by a spring whose base flow reflects upstream catchment recharge from the South Asian monsoon. Monsoon variability — increasing across the Hindu Kush Himalayan region — therefore translates directly into canal-discharge variability.

Two climate-related risks are relevant to restoration planning:

- **Inter-annual variability.** Drought years would produce reduced spring discharge at the intake, lower delivered water at the terminus, and potentially insufficient flow to maintain self-cleansing velocity (§6.3) under the canal's higher-roughness lining segments. A restored canal sized to design discharge may underperform in low-monsoon years.
- **Intra-seasonal extremes.** Increased frequency of extreme rainfall events would generate flash flows into the canal through its six cross-drainage structures (§4.5) and through informal urban storm-water inputs. The canal's capacity to convey these extremes without bank failure has not been assessed and warrants attention in the Phase 1 cross-drainage re-assessment (§10.2).

**Implication for restoration design.** Design discharge assumptions should incorporate climate-informed projections rather than historical-average values. Intake rehabilitation should include provision for monitoring spring discharge over multi-year cycles. Cross-drainage structures should be sized for projected extreme events, not historical norms.

### 7.6 Cumulative environmental risk matrix

Synthesising the four risk categories across the do-nothing, restoration-without-measures, and restoration-with-measures scenarios:

| Risk category | Do nothing | Restore without measures | Restore with measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saptapatal Pokhari contamination | Medium (ongoing decline) | **Critical** | Low |
| Vector-borne disease risk | Medium (current stagnant reaches) | High (partial flow creates new pools) | Low (continuous design flow) |
| Groundwater quality | Medium (current diffuse contamination) | High (contaminated canal infiltrates aquifer) | Medium (improving as sewage interception proceeds) |
| Climate-resilience risk | Latent (canal disused, no immediate exposure) | Medium (restored canal sized to historical Q) | Medium (mitigable through climate-informed design) |
| Cultural-heritage continuity | High (system decay) | Medium (partial recovery offset by pond contamination) | Low (system function restored coherently) |

***Table 7.1 — Cumulative environmental and public health risk matrix.***

The matrix establishes the policy core of this chapter: **the appropriate comparison is not "restoration vs. do nothing" but "restoration with parallel measures vs. restoration without parallel measures vs. do nothing".** Partial restoration without the parallel measures is the worst of the three options across most risk categories. The integrated phased plan of Chapter 10 is structured to ensure that no phase is delivered in isolation that would leave the system in this worst-case state.

### 8.4 Sensitivity discussion

The scoring framework is transparent, replicable, and useful — and it is not the only legitimate scoring framework that could be applied to the same data. Three sources of subjectivity should be acknowledged:

1. **Equal weighting across axes.** Contamination, silting, and encroachment are scored on different scales (0–4, 0–2, 0–2) but contribute additively to the composite. An alternative framework that weighted contamination more heavily — on the grounds that contamination is the most ecologically and politically consequential failure mode — would rank the upper-alignment bands lower and the 8–9 km encroachment band lower in relative terms.
2. **Coarse 1 km resolution.** Within-band variability is suppressed. A more granular scoring (e.g. 250 m segments) would resolve the perennial-to-sewage transition zone at Chainage 3+124 m as a distinct intervention boundary, separating the clean upper part of the 3–4 km band from the contaminated lower part.
3. **Band-level value assignment.** Where multiple sections within a band differ in attribute value, the area-weighted modal value was used. This is a defensible choice but not the only one; assignment by maximum value (worst-case) or by mean value (average-case) would alter the composite scores slightly.

These caveats do not undermine the matrix's central output — that the 5–7 km band is the highest priority intervention zone — but they should temper the use of the matrix for fine-grained sequencing decisions. The matrix is appropriate for first-order prioritisation; final sequencing should be informed by site-specific engineering and stakeholder considerations beyond what the matrix captures.

### 9.2 Jurisdictional boundaries

The canal alignment crosses two municipal jurisdictions, and the jurisdictional transition point is approximately at the **Chainage 5 km** boundary between the upper-alignment villages (under Godavari Municipality) and the lower-alignment urban areas (under Lalitpur Metropolitan City). The split coincides almost exactly with the onset of the most heavily contaminated reach (the 5–7 km critical band of §8.2) — meaning that the most ecologically damaging segment of the alignment is precisely the segment where coordinated cross-jurisdictional action is most needed and most difficult to organise.

Department of Irrigation maintains a national technical mandate for irrigation infrastructure under the relevant federal legislation, and was the agency responsible for the 2020 baseline study referenced throughout this report. DoI is a natural technical secretariat for any restoration framework but does not, on its own, have the urban-planning authority to address the encroachment dimensions of the problem; those are within the remit of GM, LMC, and DUDBC.

Department of Archaeology's involvement depends on whether the canal corridor is formally designated for heritage protection — currently it is not. A Department of Archaeology heritage classification, if obtained, would create a legal protection layer that constrains GM and LMC discretion on permissions affecting the corridor.

### 9.3 Legal and regulatory context

Three regulatory inconsistencies define the canal's current legal vulnerability:

1. **The 4 m buffer rule is in place but not enforced.** Building regulations across both jurisdictions notionally require a 4 m setback from the canal edge. The same regulatory framework has, in practice, permitted road construction directly over the canal at four confirmed segments. The rule and the road permissions are issued by overlapping authorities under the same legal framework.
2. **No formal heritage-corridor designation exists.** The canal is not currently classified as a heritage monument under Nepal's heritage legislation, despite its documented Licchavi-period status. Heritage classification would create legal grounds for buffer enforcement and would constrain further encroachment.
3. **No statutory water-users' association exists.** The traditional community body that maintained the canal has lapsed in most segments. There is no formal organisation with standing to represent water-users in restoration negotiations, to take on long-term maintenance responsibility, or to manage community-level disputes.

The combined effect is that the canal has neither the *de jure* legal protection that heritage classification would provide, nor the *de facto* community protection that an active water-users' association would provide. Restoration that did not address both of these structural gaps would face the same encroachment pressures that produced the current condition.

### 9.4 Community stewardship tradition

Traditionally, communities along the canal undertook annual pre-monsoon cleaning to maintain function: removing silt, clearing vegetation, repairing minor bank failures. The community labour was organised at the village level, supervised informally by elders, and timed to anticipate the seasonal flow surge. In the upper alignment (Tikabhairav, Chapagaon), traces of this practice persist; in the urbanised lower alignment, it has largely lapsed.

Three factors have weakened the tradition:

- **Loss of canal-dependent livelihoods.** Conversion of command-area agricultural land to housing has reduced the proportion of community members whose livelihoods depend directly on canal flow.
- **Anonymisation of contamination.** Sewage flowing into the canal from informal urban drainage means that physical handling of canal contents now carries health risk — a deterrent to volunteer labour.
- **Loss of supervisory structure.** The informal village-elder supervision has not been replaced by a formal water-users' association in most segments.

Re-activation of community stewardship is required for long-term sustainability of any restoration. The proposed institutional arrangement (§9.5) treats the formation of a recognised water-users' association as a precondition for transitioning from construction phase to maintenance phase.

### 9.5 Proposed institutional arrangement

A restoration framework adequate to the technical, governance, and community dimensions of the project would have the following structure:

- **Steering body: Tikabhairab Rajkulo Trust.** A formally constituted multi-stakeholder trust with rotating chair between Godavari Municipality and Lalitpur Metropolitan City; technical secretariat provided by the Department of Irrigation; observer status for the Department of Archaeology; ex-officio representation from CIUD and from the proposed Water Users' Association. The trust holds programme-level decision authority, including phasing, budget allocation, and dispute resolution.
- **Technical secretariat.** Provided by DoI. Holds technical standards, design oversight, and contractor management responsibility. Maintains the engineering documentation initiated by this report and by the recommended total-station survey.
- **Water Users' Association.** Statutorily constituted body representing communities along the alignment. Membership organised by ward; representation weighted toward currently active stewardship communities in the upper alignment. Holds maintenance responsibility from operational handover onward.
- **Legal corridor protection.** Department of Archaeology heritage classification of the canal corridor; statutory buffer-zone enforcement by GM and LMC; formal land-acquisition mechanism for resolving existing encroachments where rerouting is not feasible.

The proposed arrangement is not novel; it parallels institutional arrangements that have proved workable for comparable Kathmandu Valley heritage hydraulic restoration efforts. Its viability depends on political commitment from the two municipal governments, which is not assured.

## 10. Phased Intervention Plan

This chapter proposes a four-phase restoration sequence with cross-cutting institutional activities. The plan is **directional only**: no rupee cost figures are provided and detailed engineering specifications are out of scope for this baseline study. The plan's purpose is to establish a defensible sequence that addresses hydraulic dependencies, environmental risks, and institutional preconditions in the right order.

### 10.1 Phasing logic

The sequence of restoration phases is determined by three constraints in this order of precedence:

1. **Hydraulic dependencies.** Downstream interventions cannot be effective until upstream conditions support useful flow. Intake rehabilitation must precede or accompany downstream desilting; sewage interception must precede or accompany terminal pond connection.
2. **Environmental risk management.** No phase may be delivered in isolation that would create a worse environmental outcome than the do-nothing baseline. Specifically, restoring flow into the urban reach without parallel sewage interception is excluded as a phase boundary.
3. **Institutional preconditions.** Legal corridor protection, water-users' association formation, and steering body constitution must be in place before the first physical works begin, because their absence would expose the investment to the same encroachment pressures that produced the present condition.

Across the four phases, total construction duration is not specified (this is a function of funding flow and institutional capacity, both of which are out of scope). A conservative working planning horizon is 5–8 years from Phase 1 commencement to Phase 4 completion.

### 10.2 Phase 1 — Foundational works

**Scope.** Intake rehabilitation, sluice gate replacement, desilting of the perennial reach (0–3 km), and interception of the upstream sewage inflow at Chainage 0+524.5 m. Cross-drainage structures within the 0–2 km band re-assessed and rehabilitated as needed.

**Hydraulic objective.** Restore design discharge at the intake; restore self-cleansing flow through the perennial reach; protect the clean-water reach from the one upstream contamination source.

**Environmental objective.** Eliminate the only sewage inflow upstream of the perennial / contaminated boundary, ensuring that Phase 1 delivers clean flow into the upper canal.

**Key activities.** Structural assessment of intake works; weir desilting and re-grading; sluice gate replacement with operable mechanism and provision for stage / discharge monitoring; channel desilting downstream from intake to Chainage 3+124 m; sewage interception at SE-1 (Chainage 0+524.5 m) by diversion to nearest functional sewer or to small-scale on-site treatment; cross-drainage re-assessment.

**Institutional precondition.** Tikabhairab Rajkulo Trust constituted; DoI technical secretariat in place; corridor-protection legal status either confirmed or in formal progress; community engagement in upper-alignment villages active.

**Deliverable.** A 3 km reach of functional, clean, perennial flow from intake to Chainage 3+124 m — the rehabilitable upper canal — operating to design discharge.

### 10.3 Phase 2 — Sewage interception

**Scope.** Interception of the remaining five sewage inflow points (SE-2 to SE-6), covering chainages from 3+253.8 m to 6+888.3 m. Includes the highest-priority 5–7 km critical band identified in §8.2.

**Hydraulic objective.** Remove the principal contamination sources from the middle alignment, enabling subsequent reconstruction of physically encroached lower sections without converting them into contamination conduits.

**Environmental objective.** Reduce the cumulative contamination load that would otherwise be carried to the Saptapatal Pokhari terminus. Address the worst-case "restore without measures" scenario identified in §7.6.

**Key activities.** Detailed identification of source plots / drainage networks contributing each of SE-2 through SE-6; design of interception infrastructure (combination of municipal sewer connections, decentralised treatment, and source-side household connections to formal sewer where available); construction of interception works; commissioning and monitoring.

**Institutional precondition.** Phase 1 complete (intake operational); KUKL / municipal sewer authority cooperation secured; community consent obtained for source-side interventions.

**Deliverable.** Six sewage inflow points eliminated or routed to treatment; the middle-alignment urban reach no longer functions as a sewage conveyance channel.

### 10.4 Phase 3 — Urban reach reconstruction

**Scope.** Reconstruction of the four road-over-canal segments (Table 4.3) and resolution of the Ring Road area at Chainage 11+354.7 m. Treatment via covered-channel construction, horizontal directional drilling (HDD), or alternative routing on a segment-by-segment basis.

**Hydraulic objective.** Restore continuous open or covered conveyance from the Phase 2 reach (downstream of the last interception point) through the urban core to the Phase 4 terminus.

**Environmental objective.** Restore the canal's spatial and ecological continuity; remove the worst encroachment-driven obstruction; create conditions for the canal to function as a designated heritage corridor rather than an invisible buried channel.

**Key activities.** Segment-by-segment engineering analysis of the four road-over-canal segments; selection of intervention type per segment (covered channel within road reserve; HDD beneath road; surface re-routing along available adjacent corridor); detailed coordination with road authority and utility operators for each segment; construction and commissioning.

**Institutional precondition.** Phase 2 complete (water quality acceptable for urban-reach flow); multi-authority coordination established (road authority, utility operators, LMC); land acquisition or easement secured where required.

**Deliverable.** Continuous canal conveyance — open or covered — from the intake to the Phase 4 terminus interface; no remaining buried segments without engineered conveyance.

### 10.5 Phase 4 — Terminus and pond integration

**Scope.** Final connection of the canal to Saptapatal Pokhari; construction of small-scale water-quality treatment immediately upstream of the pond; commissioning of the integrated rajkulo–pokhari system.

**Hydraulic objective.** Restore the canal's original termination function as feeder to the pond; restore the historical hydraulic relationship between canal and pond.

**Environmental objective.** Ensure that water delivered to Saptapatal Pokhari meets the quality standards appropriate for a ritually used urban water body. Restore the cultural and hydrological function for which the system was originally designed.

**Key activities.** Detailed design of pond inflow structure (which must accommodate variable canal discharge without bank erosion of the pond); design and construction of small-scale treatment facility (likely a combination of settling and biological treatment, sized for the canal's design discharge); design of pond-level monitoring; ritual / community consultation with Saptapatal Pokhari Trust on appropriate inflow / handover arrangements; commissioning.

**Institutional precondition.** Phases 1–3 complete; water-quality monitoring confirms inflow standard achievable; Saptapatal Pokhari Trust consent obtained; long-term maintenance arrangement with Water Users' Association formalised.

**Deliverable.** The integrated rajkulo–pokhari system operating as designed; Saptapatal Pokhari receiving clean water; the canal restored to its historical function.

### 10.6 Cross-cutting activities

The four physical phases are accompanied by cross-cutting activities that run continuously from project commencement:

- **Legal corridor protection.** Department of Archaeology heritage classification of the corridor; formal statutory buffer zone enforcement by GM and LMC; encroachment register maintained.
- **Water Users' Association formation and capacity building.** Statutory constitution; ward-by-ward membership organisation; training in maintenance practice; preparation for operational handover.
- **Community engagement.** Continuous engagement with communities along the alignment, with particular attention to the urban-reach segments where canal awareness is weakest.
- **Monitoring and evaluation.** Hydrological monitoring at intake and terminus; water-quality monitoring at the six interception points and at the terminus; pond-level monitoring at Saptapatal Pokhari; periodic encroachment audits of the corridor.
- **Research follow-up.** Total-station survey (§12.1); epigraphic and archaeological work on the Tilmak question (§12.2); command-area mapping (§12.4); social survey (§12.6).

## 11. Constraints and Challenges

The constraints catalogued here are the residuals after the phased plan of Chapter 10 — the issues that remain even when restoration is sequenced correctly. They are organised by category to align with the institutional responsibilities in Chapter 9.

### 11.1 Construction challenges

The dominant construction challenge is **road-over-canal reconstruction** at the four segments documented in Table 4.3. Each requires choices among covered channel construction, horizontal directional drilling, or rerouting, with the choice depending on segment-specific factors including road category, traffic disruption tolerance, utility congestion in the road reserve, and adjacent land availability. The Ring Road area at Chainage 11+354.7 m is structurally the most demanding case: no canal currently exists at the relevant section, so a new structure or alternative route is required to complete the connection to Saptapatal Pokhari.

Secondary construction challenges include **utility conflicts** at underground crossings (the alignment intersects water supply, drainage, telecommunications, and electricity lines), and **flow management** during construction — the lower alignment must continue to convey whatever flow it currently carries even during phased reconstruction.

### 11.2 Governance challenges

The multi-level authority structure described in §9.1 is the most consequential governance challenge. The canal's cross-jurisdictional path (Godavari Municipality / Lalitpur Metropolitan City) compounds with the Department of Irrigation's national technical mandate, the Department of Urban Development and Building Construction's regulatory remit, and the Department of Archaeology's potential heritage role to create a four-or-five-authority coordination problem.

The **policy inconsistency** identified in §9.3 — the 4 m buffer rule coexisting with permissions for road-over-canal construction — is fundamental. Until it is resolved through explicit legal protection of the canal corridor, further encroachments will continue to undermine restoration investment regardless of how well the physical works are executed.

**Political will and funding continuity** are uncertain. Restoration phases extend over multiple political cycles. Without an institutional framework (the Trust structure of §9.5) that survives political turnover, phase-to-phase continuity is at risk.

### 11.3 Maintenance challenges

The most fundamental maintenance challenge is the **decline of community stewardship**. Annual pre-monsoon cleaning has lapsed in most of the alignment; sustaining a restored canal requires its re-establishment, formalised through the proposed Water Users' Association (§9.5).

**Continuous desiltation** of the rehabilitated intake is required. One-time dredging at intake commissioning is not sufficient; routine silt removal — and, crucially, transport of removed silt away from the canal banks — must be a permanent operational activity.

**Sewage-source re-emergence** is a latent maintenance risk. The six identified sewage inflow points represent the situation as of May 2022. As urbanisation continues, new inflows can emerge — particularly in the currently rural upper-alignment band — unless source-side household sewage management is implemented alongside canal-side interception. Continuous monitoring (§10.6) is required to identify and remediate new inflows before they become structural.

## 12. Research Gaps and Recommended Follow-Up

This survey establishes a baseline. It identifies six categories of follow-up work required before detailed restoration design can proceed.

### 12.1 Total station survey

**Priority: 1 (highest).** The absence of a total-station survey is the single most significant technical limitation of the present work. Per-section bed slope cannot be determined from GPS altitude data (±10–30 m vertical error) or from linear interpolation between two fixed elevation anchors.

**Required deliverable.** A continuous total-station survey from the Tikabhairav intake to the Lagankhel terminus, with bench mark establishment, longitudinal profile, and at least one cross-section per 100 m of alignment (denser in transition zones and at known break points).

**Implications of refined data.** Sections with locally flat gradients may require minor profile correction or drop-structure insertion in a restored canal; sections with locally steep gradients may need energy dissipators. The total-station data also supports calibration of the discharge-stage relationships that Phase 1 monitoring will require.

**Estimated effort.** Two-person survey crew for approximately 3–4 weeks of field work depending on access conditions in the buried segments, plus 2 weeks of data processing.

### 12.2 Epigraphic and archaeological investigation — the Tilmak question

**Priority: 2.** The 'Tilmak' nomenclature in Licchavi-period inscriptions (§2.3) is anomalous and warrants investigation. Three lines of work are required:

1. **Systematic epigraphic review** of all Licchavi inscriptions referencing 'Tilmak', with attention to the contexts in which the term appears and any internal evidence about how the term was understood at the time of inscription.
2. **Archaeological investigation** of the subsoil stratigraphy at and immediately downstream of the Tikabhairav intake structure, capable of dating the earliest extant canal works through standard sub-surface archaeological methods.
3. **Comparative toponymic analysis** of other non-Sanskritic hydraulic names in Licchavi-period records, to establish whether 'Tilmak' is anomalous or part of a recognisable pattern.

Confirmation of pre-Licchavi origins would have substantial implications for heritage classification and the international funding sources available for restoration.

### 12.3 Quantitative water quality sampling

**Priority: 3.** Field observation has identified contamination categories but not concentrations. Quantitative laboratory characterisation is required to design appropriate sewage-interception and treatment infrastructure.

**Required deliverable.** A sampling programme covering the six identified sewage inflow points (SE-1 to SE-6), the intake (clean baseline), and the Saptapatal Pokhari terminus. Parameters: BOD, COD, total suspended solids, faecal coliform counts, ammonia, nitrate, and heavy metals (lead, cadmium, chromium where industrial inflows are suspected). Seasonal repetition required (pre-monsoon, monsoon, post-monsoon, dry-season).

**Implications.** Quantitative loadings inform the sizing and treatment-process design of the Phase 4 terminus treatment facility, and the choice between source-side interception and end-of-pipe treatment in Phase 2.

### 12.4 Command area mapping

**Priority: 4.** The agricultural lands historically served by Tikabhairab Rajkulo were not systematically mapped in this survey. Identifying surviving agricultural parcels still hydraulically connected to the canal — and the households dependent on them — would quantify the irrigation benefit recoverable through restoration and provide the economic justification data required for funding proposals.

**Required deliverable.** A GIS-based command area map showing: parcels currently under cultivation; parcels with surviving canal connection; parcels converted to non-agricultural use; ownership and crop types; estimated water demand seasonally.

**Methodology.** Land-cover analysis from satellite imagery; field verification of canal-parcel connections; parcel-level interviews with surviving farming households.

### 12.5 Continuous hydrological monitoring

**Priority: 5.** Once the intake is partially rehabilitated, permanent monitoring infrastructure should be installed to characterise the canal's discharge behaviour across seasons and across multi-year climatic variation.

**Required deliverable.** Stage / discharge gauges at the intake, at the Chainage 3+124 m perennial-to-seasonal transition, and at the Saptapatal Pokhari terminus. Continuous logging with telemetry where feasible.

**Implications.** Replaces the Q = 0.10 m³/s parametric assumption of Chapter 6 with measured values; provides climate-resilience data per §7.5; informs Phase 4 treatment facility sizing.

### 12.6 Social survey

**Priority: 6.** Household-level data on communities along the alignment — current water uses, attitudes to restoration, willingness to participate in maintenance, exposure to current contamination — is required to design the community engagement and Water Users' Association elements of the restoration framework.

**Required deliverable.** A structured household survey covering communities in the seven settlements traversed (§4.1), with stratified sampling across ward-level units.

**Implications.** Provides the participation baseline against which Water Users' Association membership and stewardship activity can be planned.

## 13. Conclusion

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo presents a case in which the technical question and the institutional question diverge sharply. Technically, the canal is repairable. Its slope is more than sufficient for self-cleansing flow under any plausible discharge; its geometry is well chosen for the conditions of the Valley; its 71 % lining rate, although uneven in age and material, is broadly serviceable. The 163.14 m elevation drop along its length is a hydraulic resource that has been steadily wasted by intake siltation, downstream encroachment, and untreated sewage loading, but it has not been lost. Chapter 6 demonstrates that under unobstructed flow, the canal would self-clean across every lining type documented in the May 2022 survey. The engineering problem is unblocking the intake, intercepting six sewage inflows, reconstructing four road-buried segments, and building a small treatment facility upstream of the terminus pond. These are not small tasks, but they are bounded, definable, and within the standard repertoire of urban heritage hydraulic restoration.

Institutionally, the situation is harder. The canal crosses two municipal jurisdictions whose land-use policies have been mutually inconsistent; the legal framework that should protect the corridor has, in practice, permitted its burial; the community stewardship tradition that maintained the canal for centuries has lapsed under urbanisation; and the heritage classification that would constrain further encroachment has not been formally pursued. None of these gaps will close on their own. Closing them is a precondition for restoration, and they are addressed concretely in the stakeholder and governance framework of Chapter 9 and in the cross-cutting activities of Chapter 10.

The heritage dimension is unresolved and deserves the resources to resolve. The Tilmak question (§2.3, §12.2) cannot be settled by the field survey reported here. Three lines of work — epigraphic review, archaeological investigation, comparative toponymic analysis — are required. If the pre-Licchavi explanation is confirmed, Tikabhairab Rajkulo will rank among the oldest continuously utilised irrigation alignments documented in South Asia, with substantial implications for the international funding sources available for restoration. If it is not confirmed, the canal's Licchavi-period status and continuous-use heritage already justify the conservation framework recommended in this report. Either way the question is worth answering.

This report's specific recommendation to its sponsoring institutions is:

> Commission the **total-station survey** identified in §12.1 immediately as a stand-alone first step. Concurrently, initiate the institutional precondition work — Trust constitution, corridor legal status, Water Users' Association formation — that Phase 1 of the physical restoration depends on. The combined cost of these two parallel tracks is low compared to any phase of physical works, and their completion converts the present baseline study into an executable restoration design.

The path forward is well defined. What is now required is the institutional will to follow it.

## Appendix D — Hydraulic calculation worksheets

This appendix presents the worked examples of the four hydraulic computations underpinning Chapter 6, for reproducibility and for use by follow-up researchers.

### D.1 Bed slope

**Inputs:**
- Intake elevation: H_intake = 1,477.16 m ASL (from DoI 2020)
- Terminus elevation: H_terminus = 1,314.02 m ASL (from DoI 2020)
- GPS-measured length: L_GPS = 10,668.5 m
- AutoCAD-refined length: L_CAD = 13,158.2 m

**Computation:**
- ΔH = 1,477.16 − 1,314.02 = 163.14 m
- S_GPS = 163.14 / 10,668.5 = **0.01529 (1 in 65, 0.876°)** — primary
- S_CAD = 163.14 / 13,158.2 = 0.01240 (1 in 81, 0.710°) — sensitivity

**Formula:** Vc = 0.55 × m × D⁰·⁶⁴
**Assumptions:** m = 1.0 (standard alluvial silt)

| D (m) | D⁰·⁶⁴ | Vc = 0.55 × D⁰·⁶⁴ (m/s) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.464 | 0.255 |
| 0.40 | 0.557 | 0.306 |
| 0.50 | 0.643 | 0.354 |
| 0.60 | 0.722 | 0.397 |

### D.3 Lacey's regime slope

**Formula:** S₀ = f^(5/3) / (3340 × Q^(1/6))
**Assumptions:** f = 1.0, Q = 0.10 m³/s

- Q^(1/6) = 0.10^(1/6) = 0.681
- 3340 × 0.681 = 2,275
- S₀ = 1 / 2,275 = **0.000440**
- Ratio S / S₀:
  - Primary (S = 0.01529): 0.01529 / 0.000440 = **34.8 ≈ 35**
  - Sensitivity (S = 0.01240): 0.01240 / 0.000440 = 28.2 ≈ 28

### D.4 Manning's equation

- A = 0.50 × 0.40 = 0.20 m²
- P = 0.50 + 2 × 0.40 = 1.30 m
- R = 0.20 / 1.30 = 0.154 m
- R^(2/3) = 0.287
- S^(1/2) at primary slope (S = 0.01529): 0.1236

## Appendix F — Recommended next-phase survey protocol

This appendix outlines the methodology recommended for the priority-1 follow-up activity (§12.1) — the total-station survey from intake to terminus.

### F.1 Objectives

To establish per-section bed slope with engineering-grade vertical accuracy; to establish a benchmark network suitable for downstream phase-by-phase construction control; to generate longitudinal-profile and cross-section data sufficient to support the Phase 1 intake design and the Phase 3 urban-reach reconstruction.

### F.2 Method

- **Equipment:** Reflectorless total station (e.g. Leica TS06, Trimble S5, or equivalent), tripod, prism set, GPS receiver for benchmark anchoring.
- **Benchmarks:** Establish a permanent reference benchmark at the intake (anchored on the DoI 2020 elevation 1,477.16 m) and at the terminus (DoI 2020 elevation 1,314.02 m). Establish intermediate temporary benchmarks every 1 km of alignment.
- **Profile:** Continuous longitudinal profile shots at not more than 25 m intervals along the canal centreline. Denser shots at structural transitions (intake works, cross-drainage structures, hume-pipe transitions, road crossings).
- **Cross-sections:** Minimum one full cross-section per 100 m of alignment. Denser cross-sections in the perennial reach (every 50 m, 0–3 km) and at all six sewage inflow POIs.
- **Buried sections:** Where access permits, profile through manhole access; where access does not permit, fall back to inference from upstream and downstream visible sections and flag the gap in deliverables.

### F.3 Deliverables

- Continuous longitudinal profile from intake to terminus, with per-section bed slope.
- Cross-section catalogue with at least 130 cross-sections.
- Updated chainage register cross-referenced to this report's Chainage values.
- Benchmark register with permanent reference monumentation.
- GeoPackage / DWG export for integration with the existing alignment GIS dataset.

### F.4 Estimated effort

Two-person survey crew, 3–4 weeks of field work plus 2 weeks of data processing and deliverable preparation. The buried-section subset (3,182.9 m, per §4.2) may require additional time depending on access arrangements with the relevant road authorities.
