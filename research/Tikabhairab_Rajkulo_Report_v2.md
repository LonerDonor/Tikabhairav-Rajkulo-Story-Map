## Executive Summary

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo is a hill-fed canal that runs about 11.8 km along its main line, from the Tikabhairav intake in the forested hills south of Lalitpur to Lagankhel, where it historically fed Saptapatal Pokhari. The CAD alignment drawn for this study, which includes some branch loops, measures 13.2 km. The canal is one of the principal hydraulic alignments of the Kathmandu Valley and, on the basis of epigraphic evidence summarised in Chapter 2, may represent a substantially older hydraulic tradition than its Licchavi-period documentation alone would suggest.

This report presents the findings of a GPS-based field survey carried out between 4 and 11 May 2022, on five field days. The survey dataset holds 107 canal sections (95 walked and GPS-recorded, 12 known only from the CAD drawings), 68 georeferenced points of interest (POIs) and 244 photographs. Every figure in this report is taken from that dataset unless another source is named.

**What the survey shows:**

- **The canal carries water year-round only in its first 3.1 km.** Perennial flow was recorded in every section with a flow record from the intake to Chainage 3+124 m: 26.5 % of the 11,773 m main line. The next 317 m was not walked. From 3+441 m onward, every section with a flow record was seasonal.
- **Below that point, sewage and waste are the norm.** Sewage or waste dumping was recorded in 30 sections, 3.55 km of channel (36 % of the walked length). Upstream of 3+124 m it appears in 2 of the 48 sections with a water-quality record; downstream of 3+441 m, in 28 of 34.
- **Contamination starts within the first kilometre.** A contamination outflow (POI 1) enters at about 0+344 m, and sewage was recorded in the water at 0+509–0+531 m, beside a drain (POI 4). The two other contamination points, both sewage lines, are at about 6.9 km (POIs 42 and 43).
- **Silting is almost universal.** It was recorded in 67 of the 76 sections with a silting record (88 %): 45 of 46 upstream of 3+124 m and 22 of 30 downstream. The heaviest deposit is at the intake, where the weir is filled with silt and the sluice gate does not work.
- **The canal is still largely a lined channel.** 81 of the 83 sections with a lining record are lined, although 12 field notes record lining on one side only. The median rectangular section is 60 cm wide and 35 cm deep.
- **Parts of the canal have disappeared from view.** In 9 walked sections (1.73 km) the canal could not be seen, 8 of them between 3.6 and 8.4 km, and 10 field notes describe it running underground or under a road. The last 2.1 km to Lagankhel, beyond 9+650 m, was not walked.

A restoration priority matrix, scoring each 1 km band on recorded contamination, silting and encroachment, ranks the **4–6 km and 7–8 km bands as critical**. The 9–10 km band also scores critical, on the evidence of a single surveyed section. The upper reach (0–3 km), although the least contaminated, needs intake rehabilitation and early action on the two wastewater entries in its first kilometre to protect the canal's only remaining clean water.

Restoring the canal as a feeder for Saptapatal Pokhari would depend on four things: rehabilitating the intake and sluice gate; stopping the wastewater that enters the canal; reopening or replacing the underground and road-covered stretches; and treating the water before it reaches the pond. The institutional preconditions — corridor protection, a water users' association, and a steering body spanning both municipalities — matter as much as the physical works.

This report does not specify the engineering design of those interventions. It provides the empirical baseline required before such a design can responsibly proceed: alignment, condition catalogue, water quality, a summary of what the survey data show, environmental and public health risk framing, a restoration priority matrix, a stakeholder framework and a phased intervention plan. The most important technical follow-up is a level or total-station survey of the canal bed (§12.1). The most important institutional follow-up is a legal protection corridor for the canal across both Godavari Municipality and Lalitpur Metropolitan City.

The path forward is well defined. What is now required is the institutional will to follow it.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background — hydraulic heritage of the Kathmandu Valley

Water management has been central to settled life in the Kathmandu Valley since at least the early historic period. The Valley's three medieval cities — Kathmandu, Lalitpur, and Bhaktapur — developed in close hydrological relationship with the surrounding upland watersheds, sustained by an integrated infrastructure of canals, ponds, fields, and below-ground stone-spout systems that operated together as a single hydrologic mechanism. Surface water from hill springs and rain-fed catchments was conveyed through hill-fed canals (*rajkulo*) to irrigate the surrounding farmland and to recharge the urban ponds (*pokhari*); infiltration from canals and ponds replenished the shallow aquifer; that aquifer in turn discharged at controlled points through the stone-spout (*hiti*) network that supplied drinking water to the historic cores. The fact that this was a coherent system — not a collection of independent features — has been documented at length by Padma Sundar Joshi's *Hiti Pranali* and is a precondition for understanding the present study.

The rajkulo–pokhari–hiti system has, in the last fifty years, experienced near-systemic disruption. Rapid urbanisation across the southern Valley has converted irrigation command areas to housing plots, narrowed canal corridors, buried canal alignments beneath roads, and connected domestic and storm drainage directly to surviving canal channels. The result is a Valley in which the historic ponds are drying, the historic hiti are running thin or dry, and the canal alignments that once knit the system together are silted, contaminated, or invisible. The loss is hydrological, ecological, and cultural at once.

### 1.2 The Tikabhairab Rajkulo

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo is one of the principal canals of this system. It originates at the Tikabhairav intake on the Lele Khola (approximately 27.574°N, 85.318°E; 1,477.16 m ASL) in the forested hills south of Lalitpur and runs, in its historical configuration, about 11.8 km north along its main line through the settlements of Chapagaon, Pyangaon, Thecho, Sunakothi, and Dholahiti to terminate at Saptapatal Pokhari in Lagankhel (1,314.02 m ASL). Both elevations are benchmarks from the Department of Irrigation's 2020 Rajkulo study; the fall along the canal between them was not measured in this survey.

The canal is documented in Licchavi-period inscriptions and was most recently formally restored, in part, in 2019 BS (approximately 1962 CE). It served three interconnected functions historically: irrigation of the southern Lalitpur hinterland; recharge of Saptapatal Pokhari at the Lagankhel terminus; and, through Saptapatal Pokhari and aquifer pathways, indirect support of the hiti network in Lalitpur's historic core. As of the May 2022 field survey, none of these functions is operating at scale.

### 1.3 Study rationale

Saptapatal Pokhari at Lagankhel — the canal's historical terminus — has experienced sharp declines in water level over the past two decades, attributable both to reduced surface-water inflow (the canal itself) and to falling groundwater levels in the surrounding aquifer. Restoring Tikabhairab Rajkulo as a feeder to the pokhari has been a recurring proposal in municipal and donor discussions but has not, to date, been preceded by the kind of systematic baseline assessment that would allow the engineering, governance, and environmental dimensions of restoration to be reasoned about in concert. This report is intended to fill that gap.

The May 2022 field survey was designed to produce a single, internally consistent baseline dataset documenting the canal's full surveyed alignment at the level of detail required for restoration planning: section-by-section condition, water quality, lining material, geometry, and flow regime, all with GPS-referenced positioning. The accompanying desktop analyses — a summary of what the data show, risk framing, and priority scoring — convert that dataset into the planning artefacts (data summary, environmental risk framing, restoration priority matrix, phased intervention plan) that the report's recommended next steps depend on.

### 1.4 Objectives

The study has six objectives:

1. Document the physical condition, geometry, lining material, and water quality of the full surveyed canal alignment from the Tikabhairav intake to the Rajdal Barrack / Lagankhel terminus.
2. Identify the canal's flow regime (perennial, seasonal, interrupted, disturbed) and map the spatial distribution of silting, contamination, and encroachment.
3. Summarise what the survey data show about flow, contamination, silting, lining and visibility along the canal, using only the recorded section attributes, field notes and points of interest.
4. Construct a quantitative restoration priority matrix scoring each 1 km band of the alignment against contamination, silting, and encroachment severity.
5. Assess the engineering, governance, and maintenance challenges associated with restoring the canal as a feeder for Saptapatal Pokhari, and the environmental and public health risks of doing so without parallel sewage management.
6. Document the canal's heritage significance, including the epigraphic evidence whose interpretation is summarised in §2.3, and identify research priorities for future study.

### 1.5 Scope and study area

The study covers the main alignment of Tikabhairab Rajkulo from intake to terminus, together with the parallel branches recorded at about 8.1 km (sections 92–93) and an alternative lower route (sections 99–102, ending at Chainage 11+590.6 m), surveyed for evaluation as alternative routes. Administratively, the alignment crosses two jurisdictions — Godavari Municipality in the upper and middle reaches and Lalitpur Metropolitan City in the lower urban reaches — a fact whose implications for restoration governance are taken up in Chapter 9.

The study explicitly *excludes* the following, which are identified as priorities for follow-up work:

- Bed-slope measurement, which requires a level or total-station survey (§3.8, §12.1);
- Laboratory quantification of water quality at the contamination points and wastewater entries identified in §5.4 and at the terminus (§12.3);
- Command-area mapping of the agricultural parcels historically served by the canal (§12.4);
- Continuous hydrological monitoring of intake discharge across seasons (§12.5);
- Detailed household-level social survey of communities along the alignment (§12.6).

The Tikabhairav intake structure itself is included only at the level of visual inspection; a full structural assessment of the intake works is identified as a Phase 1 engineering deliverable in Chapter 10, not as part of this baseline survey.

### 1.6 Report structure

The report is organised into thirteen chapters and six appendices. Chapters 1–2 establish background and heritage context. Chapter 3 documents methodology. Chapters 4–5 present the field-condition findings (structure, alignment, flow, water quality). Chapter 6 summarises what the survey data show along the canal. Chapter 7 presents the new environmental and public health risk framework. Chapter 8 presents the quantitative restoration priority matrix. Chapter 9 presents the stakeholder and governance framework. Chapter 10 presents the phased intervention plan. Chapters 11–12 catalogue residual challenges and research gaps. Chapter 13 concludes.

## 2. Heritage Significance and Historical Context

### 2.1 The integrated hydraulic system of the Kathmandu Valley

The rajkulo, the pokhari, and the hiti form three components of a single hydrological apparatus that has long been bound up with the spatial and ritual organisation of the Kathmandu Valley's urban settlements. The components were not independently designed; their geometric, hydraulic, and elevational relationships were calibrated to one another and to the field systems and groundwater regime in which they were embedded.

A rajkulo's alignment was determined by two competing constraints: the elevation required to deliver flow under gravity to its command area and to its terminal pokhari; and the topographic feasibility of the alignment between intake and terminus. The rajkulo intake itself was located where surface flow could be reliably abstracted year-round — typically a spring-fed or low-order stream at sufficient elevation that command-area delivery was possible by gravity alone. Tikabhairav meets both criteria: the intake takes water from the Lele Khola at 1,477.16 m ASL, situated 163.14 m above Saptapatal Pokhari (1,314.02 m ASL), according to the Department of Irrigation's 2020 benchmarks.

The pokhari at the canal's terminus served a dual function. It buffered the canal's seasonal variability — storing water during high-flow periods for use during low-flow ones — and it acted as a recharge reservoir for the local aquifer through bed infiltration. The downstream hiti network in the historic core drew on that aquifer through a system of bevelled spout outlets at controlled depths.

The system is therefore tightly coupled. A failing canal does not simply lose its irrigation function; it also drains the pokhari that it fed and, in time, the aquifer that the pokhari recharged, and ultimately the hiti that the aquifer fed. The reverse is equally true: restoring the canal restores the pond, which restores aquifer recharge, which restores the hiti. The system can fail or recover as a unit. The case for restoring Tikabhairab Rajkulo accordingly cannot be made purely on irrigation grounds; it must be made — and is made in this report — on integrated hydrological-cultural grounds.

### 2.2 The Licchavi hydraulic record

The Licchavi dynasty (c. 4th–9th century CE) provides the earliest substantial epigraphic record of formal canal administration in the Kathmandu Valley. Licchavi inscriptions, surviving on stone pillars and copper plates at numerous Valley locations, document royal patronage of canal construction and maintenance; the imposition of canal-related taxes; the regulation of water distribution among command-area villages; and the appointment of officials responsible for canal upkeep. Several inscriptions explicitly reference the canal known today as Tikabhairab Rajkulo, attesting to its integration into Licchavi-period agricultural administration.

The administrative and religious language of the Licchavi state was Sanskrit. Inscriptions, royal orders, land grants, and infrastructure designations of the period are recorded in Sanskrit, and the technical nomenclature applied to formally state-built infrastructure under Licchavi patronage is, with rare exceptions, Sanskritic in origin. This is consistent with the broader pattern of administrative-language adoption documented across South Asian early-medieval polities.

### 2.3 The Tilmak question

Against this background, the Licchavi-period name for the canal that this report concerns presents an interpretive problem worth taking seriously. As documented in Padma Sundar Joshi's *Hiti Pranali*, Licchavi inscriptions refer to the canal by the term **'Tilmak'** *[CITATION: Hiti Pranali, page TK — to be inserted once the specific passage is located]* — a word with no traceable Sanskrit root.

A Sanskrit-administering dynasty whose other infrastructure designations are Sanskritic would be expected, on the prior pattern, to assign a Sanskritic name to a state canal. The persistence of a non-Sanskritic name in formal royal records is therefore anomalous and invites explanation. At least three explanations can be considered, each with different implications for the canal's heritage classification.

**Explanation 1: Inheritance of name (and probably alignment) from the pre-Licchavi Kirat period.** Under this reading, the canal — or at least its established course and local designation — predates the arrival of Licchavi administration and was inherited from the Kirat communities who were the attested pre-Licchavi inhabitants of the Valley. Licchavi inscriptions would then record an existing toponym rather than bestow a new one. This explanation is consistent with the general pattern observed elsewhere in South Asia, in which incoming administrations have selectively absorbed and institutionalised the hydraulic practices of predecessor communities.

**Explanation 2: Borrowing from a local non-Sanskrit hydraulic lexicon contemporaneous with the Licchavi period.** Under this reading, 'Tilmak' was a vernacular term in use among local communities during the Licchavi period without itself implying pre-Licchavi origins. The inscription would then reflect Licchavi adoption of vernacular usage for what may have been a Licchavi-era construction.

**Explanation 3: Scribal preservation of a vernacular designation despite Sanskritic administrative practice.** Under this reading, 'Tilmak' could have entered formal records as a fixed proper noun whose original meaning was no longer transparent at the time of inscription, with no implication about either pre-Licchavi origin or the timing of construction.

These three explanations are not exhaustive and are not mutually exclusive; the first and the second, for example, are compatible if a Kirat-period name was retained as vernacular usage into the Licchavi period. The point of distinguishing them is that the three carry different evidentiary burdens and different conservation implications. Explanation 1, if confirmed, would place Tikabhairab Rajkulo among the oldest continuously utilised irrigation alignments documented in South Asia, with substantial implications for international heritage classification. Explanations 2 and 3 leave the canal's significance considerable but bounded by its Licchavi-period documentation.

The hypothesis cannot be resolved by the field survey reported here. Resolution requires three lines of work, identified in §12.2 as priority follow-up research: (i) systematic epigraphic review of all Licchavi inscriptions referencing 'Tilmak', with attention to the contexts in which the term appears (irrigation orders, land grants, religious endowments) and any internal evidence about how the term was understood at the time of inscription; (ii) archaeological investigation of the subsoil stratigraphy at and immediately downstream of the Tikabhairav intake structure, capable of dating the earliest extant canal works; and (iii) comparative toponymic analysis of other non-Sanskritic hydraulic names in Licchavi-period records, to establish whether 'Tilmak' is anomalous or part of a recognisable pattern.

### 2.4 Heritage classification implications

The case for treating Tikabhairab Rajkulo as a heritage asset under Nepal's monument-protection framework does not depend on resolution of the Tilmak question. Its documented Licchavi-period status, the 2019 BS / 1962 CE intake restoration, and its role in a continuously functioning Valley hydraulic system already establish the case for formal protection of the canal corridor under national heritage legislation. Resolution of the Tilmak question would strengthen the case for international recognition and may, depending on the outcome, expand the funding sources available for restoration. The present report does not assume any particular outcome on this question but treats the question as deserving the resources to answer.

The practical consequence — taken up in Chapter 9 — is that any restoration framework should treat the Tikabhairab Rajkulo corridor as a heritage asset deserving the kind of legal protection that prevents continued encroachment, regardless of how the Tilmak question is ultimately resolved.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Research design

The study combines two methods: a structured field survey of the canal's full alignment, conducted in May 2022, and a desktop analysis of the field-collected data, including a data summary, priority scoring and risk framing. The two methods are not sequential but iterative: early analysis informed mid-survey refinements to data capture, and final-stage analyses (priority matrix, risk framework) drew on both field observation and structured re-examination of the survey dataset.

The field survey adopts a section-based unit of analysis. Each surveyed section is a contiguous segment of canal with uniform attributes (lining, geometry, flow regime, water quality category, silting status). Section boundaries are drawn where any of these attributes change. The survey dataset holds **107 sections**: 95 walked and recorded with GPS, and 12 known only from the CAD drawings, which were not walked. The sections cover the main line from the intake to Lagankhel, the parallel branches at about 8.1 km and the alternative lower route ending at 11+590.6 m.

A parallel record of points of interest (POIs) was kept for discrete features not suited to section-based representation. A total of **68 POIs** were recorded with GPS coordinates, photographs and supplementary observations: 49 observation points, 7 cross-drainage points, 4 encroachment sites, 3 contamination points, 3 vulnerable zones and 2 intake structures (weirs).

### 3.2 Field survey protocol

A structured data-collection flowchart guided surveyors from initial section identification to attribute classification. The flowchart sequence: stretch code and street name recording → in-situ visibility check → lined / unlined status and material identification → geometric section classification (rectangular, hume pipe / circular) → flow regime classification (perennial, seasonal, interrupted, unknown) → silting status → secondary canal presence → water quality classification (river water, storm water, sewage, waste dumping; multi-select) → photographic documentation.

Canal dimensions (width, depth, lining thickness where measurable) were measured using a steel tape. Where vegetation or silt accumulation prevented direct measurement, dimensions were estimated from visible structural elements and flagged in field notes; in heavily silted sections, only top width and overall structural form could be reliably recorded. Manual data collection via pen-and-paper was maintained alongside digital entry on the GPS-based field application for verification and redundancy.

Water quality classification was qualitative and observational. The four categories (river water, storm water, sewage, waste dumping) were recorded as multi-select values per section because contamination in the field was frequently multi-source — a section might carry simultaneous sewage, storm-water, and solid-waste inputs from adjacent informal urban drainage. No laboratory sampling was conducted in this survey phase; quantitative water-quality characterisation is identified as a follow-up requirement in §12.3.

### 3.3 Spatial data acquisition

Canal alignment geometry was recorded using a GPS-based mobile field-data-collection application (Mergin Maps / Input App) running on a smartphone, with a Google Satellite imagery layer for in-field reference. The canal was tracked as a series of GPS line features, one per surveyed section. Where a dedicated GPS device was available, its readings were preferred over smartphone GPS at section boundary points to reduce horizontal positional error. The field work took five days — 4, 5, 8, 10 and 11 May 2022 — and produced 244 photographs.

The horizontal accuracy of consumer-grade GPS receivers under partial canopy cover is typically ±3–10 m, which is adequate for the alignment-mapping purposes of this study. Their vertical accuracy is much worse, so GPS-derived elevation is not used in this report. The only elevations cited are the two benchmarks of the Department of Irrigation's 2020 Rajkulo study: 1,477.16 m ASL at the intake and 1,314.02 m ASL at the terminus.

### 3.4 Length measurement and reconciliation

Four length figures appear in this report and must be distinguished:

- **Main-line chainage: 11,773.3 m.** The distance along the main line from the intake to the Lagankhel terminus. Chainage is recorded at the end of each section; the alternative lower route ends at 11,590.6 m.
- **Walked length: 9,994.0 m.** The sum of the lengths of the 95 walked sections, including the parallel branches. Percentages of "walked length" in this report are of this figure.
- **All 107 sections: 13,232.7 m.** The walked sections plus the 12 CAD-only sections (3,238.7 m), which were not walked: one between 3+124 and 3+441 m, and the rest beyond 9+650 m.
- **CAD alignment: 13,158.2 m.** The length of the alignment in the AutoCAD drawings prepared for this study, which include stretches inferred from satellite imagery and some branch loops. It is quoted for reference only.

### 3.5 Data processing

All figures in Chapters 4–8 are computed from the survey dataset as published with the Living Heritage Map (`site/data.js`). The scripts that produce the figures and the priority scores are in the project repository (`scripts/build_figures.py`). The rules used throughout:

- **Main line.** The intake-to-Lagankhel path. Parallel branches and the alternative lower route are included in walked-length totals but not in figures along the chainage.
- **"With a record."** Sections whose field is blank are left out of the shares quoted for that field. For example, 76 sections have a silting record, so the silting share is out of 76.
- **Condition.** Each section is assigned one of six classes: *Lost* when the canal was not seen; *Critical* when sewage or waste dumping and silting were both recorded; *Poor* for sewage or waste dumping without silting; *Fair* for silting alone; *Good* otherwise; and *No data* when nothing was recorded (including the 12 CAD-only sections).
- **POI chainage.** Each POI is placed at the chainage of the nearest point on the nearest walked section.

### 3.6 Restoration priority scoring methodology

The restoration priority matrix in Chapter 8 scores each one-kilometre band of the main line on three parameters, each weighted equally:

- **Contamination score (0–4).** 0 = river water only; 1 = storm water, without sewage or waste dumping; 2 = sewage or waste dumping; 4 = sewage and waste dumping together. The scale is non-linear because combined contamination is more severe, by ecological and remediation-cost measures, than either source alone. Sections without a water-quality record are left out.
- **Silting score (0–2).** 0 = silting absent; 1 = not recorded (including canal not seen); 2 = silting present.
- **Encroachment score (0–2).** 2 = canal not seen, or recorded as running underground or under a road; 1 = a field note records a retaining wall, buildings, plotting or disruption by construction, or an Encroachment POI lies on the section; 0 = otherwise.

Each band's score on each parameter is the length-weighted mean over the walked main-line sections in the band, with sections split at band edges. Bands with less than 500 m walked are not scored.

Composite band scores are summed (maximum = 8) and classified into four priority tiers:
- **Critical** ≥ 5.0 — immediate restoration intervention required;
- **High** 3.5 to < 5.0 — intervention required in early restoration phases;
- **Medium** 2.0 to < 3.5 — intervention required but not immediately critical;
- **Low** < 2.0 — minor maintenance only.

The scoring framework is deliberately simple. Its limitations — equal weighting across three different qualitative axes; coarse 1 km resolution; encroachment evidence that depends on what the field notes happened to record — are discussed in §8.4. The framework's purpose is to support sequencing decisions in restoration planning, not to replace detailed engineering assessment of individual reaches.

### 3.8 Survey limitations

The following limitations are identified and should be borne in mind when interpreting the report's findings:

1. **No level survey.** Bed slope was not measured, so this report draws no hydraulic conclusions; a level or total-station survey of the canal bed is needed (§12.1).
2. **Unwalked and hidden stretches.** The 12 CAD-only sections (3.24 km) were not walked: one between 3+124 and 3+441 m and the rest beyond 9+650 m. In a further 9 walked sections (1.73 km) the canal could not be seen. Nothing is recorded for these stretches beyond their position.
3. **Heavily silted sections.** In sections where silt deposits and overgrown vegetation prevented physical measurement, dimensions were estimated or omitted; the section attributes for these reaches are less reliable than for clean reaches.
4. **Inconsistent original lining material.** Partial reconstruction of the canal over different historical periods means that the original construction material is not consistently identifiable along the alignment; current material (as observed in 2022) is recorded but does not always correspond to original.
5. **One-sided lining variation.** Twelve field notes record sections lined on one side only (typically against a natural rock face), with dimensions varying along the same section. Where this is the case, the recorded dimensions reflect the dominant configuration and may not describe the section fully.
6. **Qualitative water quality.** No laboratory sampling. Water quality classification is observational and represents conditions at the time of survey (May 2022; pre-monsoon).
7. **Incomplete records.** Not every attribute was recorded for every walked section: 83 sections have a lining record, 83 a flow record (one of them 'unknown'), 82 a water-quality record and 76 a silting record. Shares in this report are of the sections with a record (§3.5).
8. **Origin period not determinable.** The canal's earliest construction period cannot be inferred from this survey; epigraphic and archaeological investigation is required (§12.2).

Despite these limitations, the dataset collected is the most systematic baseline survey of the canal completed to date and is sufficient for the planning purposes for which this report is intended.

## 4. Canal Structure and Alignment

### 4.1 Alignment and settlements traversed

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo originates at the Tikabhairav intake structure (Chainage 0+000 m, 1,477.16 m ASL) and runs north through a sequence of villages and peri-urban settlements before reaching the Lagankhel terminus (Chainage 11+773.3 m, 1,314.02 m ASL; both elevations DoI 2020). The settlements traversed, in approximate chainage order, are:

- **Tikabhairav** (0–1 km) — intake village; hill catchment.
- **Chapagaon** (1–3 km) — established rural settlement; canal still in semi-traditional form.
- **Pyangaon** (3–4 km) — rural-to-peri-urban transition; the perennial-to-seasonal flow boundary (3+124 to 3+441 m) falls near the upstream edge of this settlement.
- **Thecho** (4–5 km) — peri-urban; the canal passes under a road at 4+227–4+781 m (sections 65 and 67).
- **Sunakothi** (5–7 km) — heavily urbanising; continuous sewage, storm water and waste dumping.
- **Dholahiti** (7–9 km) — peri-urban; the canal is underground and not seen for 488 m (section 90, 7+646–8+134 m).
- **Lagankhel** (9–12 km) — urban; not walked beyond 9+650 m. The CAD drawings carry the line to the terminus at or near Saptapatal Pokhari.

A short alternative lower route ends at Naricha (Chainage 11+590.6 m).

Chapagaon, Thecho and Sunakothi are historic settlements along the canal, and canal and settlements have long depended on each other: the communities maintained the canal, and it supplied their fields, drinking water, ponds and hiti. Whether the settlements grew up around the canal, or the canal was dug to serve settlements already there, remains an open question that the epigraphic and archaeological work proposed in §12.2 could help resolve.

81 of the 83 sections with a lining record are lined, totalling 7,794 m (78.0 % of the walked length). The two unlined sections total 196 m (sections 39 and 63). The survey's lining-material field (GeoPackage `Survey_new`) shows how the material changes along the main line:

Periodic annual cleaning is undertaken by local communities before the monsoon. The removed silt is, however, typically piled along the canal banks rather than being transported away — a practice that, while well-intentioned, gives several sections the misleading appearance from above of an unlined or collapsed canal even where the lining is intact beneath the silt pile.

### 4.4 Intake structure at Tikabhairav

The intake structure at Tikabhairav, which diverts water from the Lele Khola and was formally restored in 2019 BS (approximately 1962 CE), is heavily silted. The weir is filled with sediment to a depth restricting passive flow, and the sluice gate that historically controlled discharge into the canal is non-functional: it cannot regulate flow even when intake water is present. The survey team recorded silt 1.5–2.0 m deep in the channel immediately downstream, decreasing further downstream; silting was recorded in all of the first six sections (sections 1–6, the first 84 m of channel). The intake is the canal's only inlet. The surveyors' labels for these sections ("Intake", "intake 2", "Intake3") mark consecutive stretches of the one channel below it, not separate intakes: along the canal they run in the order 1, 5, 2, 3, 4, 6.

The intake is the primary focus of Phase 1 restoration (§10.2).

2. **The clean upper canal ends at 3+124 m.** Phase 1 restoration (intake and upper canal) can deliver clean water to the upper reach without immediate concern about contamination from the lower alignment, provided the two entries in the first kilometre are dealt with. Phase 1 is therefore a meaningful intervention on its own merits, not contingent on completion of Phase 2.
3. **Beyond 9+650 m, water quality is unknown.** The canal was not walked there, so its condition and water content cannot be verified until access is restored. Phase 3 should include provision for water-quality monitoring access at several chainages.

## 7. Environmental and Public Health Risk Assessment

The condition data in Chapters 4–6 show that restoration would mean repairing an existing, mostly lined channel rather than building new infrastructure. That presumes restoration is desirable. This chapter examines the environmental and public health consequences of restoration — and of non-restoration — and identifies the risk-management measures that must accompany any physical intervention.

The framing here is that restoration is itself a risk-creating activity, not solely a risk-reducing one. Restoring flow through a canal whose lower alignment carries sewage or waste in 28 of the 34 sections with a water-quality record would convert it from a degraded local environment into a contamination channel terminating in a culturally significant pond. The case for restoration depends on the credibility of the parallel measures that prevent this outcome.

### 7.1 Risk framework

Four risk categories are considered:

- **Water quality risk:** transport of contaminants from wastewater entries to downstream receptors, particularly Saptapatal Pokhari at the terminus.
- **Vector-borne disease risk:** stagnant water in seasonal and disturbed reaches providing breeding habitat for mosquito vectors (*Aedes*, *Culex*, *Anopheles*) at the urban interface.
- **Groundwater risk:** contaminant infiltration from the canal to the shallow aquifer that supplies the historic Lalitpur hiti network and adjacent shallow domestic wells.
- **Climate-resilience risk:** vulnerability of the restored canal to inter-annual variability in monsoon precipitation and the reliability of the Lele Khola's flow at the intake under projected climate trajectories.

Each category is assessed for current condition (do-nothing baseline), and for two scenarios: restoration without parallel water-management measures, and restoration with parallel measures. The point of distinguishing the two restoration scenarios is to make explicit the dependence of restoration benefit on parallel investments.

### 7.2 Contamination risk to Saptapatal Pokhari

Saptapatal Pokhari at Lagankhel is a culturally significant urban pond whose water levels have declined sharply over the past two decades. Its restoration would be one of the principal benefits claimed for any Tikabhairab Rajkulo restoration project. Yet under current canal conditions, restoring flow to the pond without parallel sewage management would carry the sewage and waste recorded along 3.55 km of the canal directly into the pond.

The scale of the problem is set by the survey: sewage, storm water and waste dumping were recorded together along 2,366.7 m of walked channel (14 sections between 4+417 and 9+650 m), and sewage or waste in 30 sections in all. Whatever the discharge, that load would end in the pond. The pond would not act as a buffer; it would act as a sink.

The downstream consequences would include:

- **Ecological degradation of the pond.** Eutrophication from nutrient loading; loss of aquatic biota; algal bloom risk during low-flow periods.
- **Public health risk.** The pond is used for ritual immersion and for proximate domestic water uses; direct human contact with sewage-loaded water would create acute health risk in the surrounding ward.
- **Groundwater contamination cascade.** The pond functions as an aquifer recharge feature. Contaminated pond water would infiltrate to the shallow aquifer that feeds the Lalitpur hiti network — converting a restoration project for the rajkulo into a contamination pathway for the hiti.
- **Reputational and political risk.** Visible contamination of a heritage pond in a flagship restoration project would compromise public confidence in heritage hydraulic restoration efforts more broadly across the Valley.

**Implication for restoration design.** Interception of the wastewater entering the canal (§5.4), together with a small-scale water-quality treatment facility immediately upstream of the pond, is non-optional. Restoring flow without these measures is not a partial restoration; it is the substitution of one form of damage for another.

### 7.3 Vector-borne disease risk in stagnant sections

The canal's seasonal reach — every section with a flow record from 3+441 m onward, 4.99 km of walked channel (§5.1) — can hold stagnant or near-stagnant water for extended periods, particularly in the post-monsoon and dry-season transition. Stagnant water in urban-interface settings supports breeding of *Aedes aegypti* (dengue / chikungunya vector), *Culex* species (West Nile / Japanese encephalitis vectors), and *Anopheles* species (malaria vectors in lower-altitude reaches).

Kathmandu Valley has experienced expanding dengue prevalence over the past decade, with documented seasonal outbreaks in Lalitpur. The canal's lower alignment runs through high-density urban wards whose populations are directly exposed to any vector population sustained by canal-borne breeding habitat.

The risk is bidirectional with respect to restoration:

- **Status quo (no restoration).** Stagnant pools in the disturbed reach provide ongoing breeding habitat. Vector density is sustained at a level set by the current ratio of pooled to flowing water across seasons.
- **Restoration without intake rehabilitation.** Worst case. Some pools are refreshed but others, where infill or partial sewage flow creates new stagnant zones, become more rather than less hospitable to vector breeding.
- **Full restoration (intake rehabilitated; sewage intercepted; flow restored).** Best case. Continuous flow leaves fewer standing pools along the alignment, and vector habitat shrinks accordingly.

**Implication for restoration design.** Vector-breeding risk reinforces the case for intake rehabilitation in Phase 1 (§10.2): restoring year-round flow reduces the standing water in which vectors breed. Partial restoration that leaves the lower alignment in a half-flowing state could be counterproductive.

### 7.4 Groundwater recharge implications

The hydraulic system schematic of §2.1 establishes that the canal contributes, through Saptapatal Pokhari and through direct bed infiltration along its alignment, to the shallow aquifer that feeds the Lalitpur hiti network. The implications are twofold and operate in tension.

**Recharge benefit.** A restored canal carrying perennial flow would substantially increase shallow-aquifer recharge across the southern Lalitpur urban area, supporting both the historic hiti network and the shallow domestic wells that supplement piped water supply in the same neighbourhoods. The magnitude of this benefit is not quantified in the present study; it would depend on canal-bed permeability, water-table position, and the volume of leakage through unlined and partially lined sections. Quantification is identified as a follow-up activity in §12.5.

**Contamination cascade risk.** The same infiltration pathway that delivers recharge benefit also delivers contaminants. Sewage-loaded water infiltrating from canal sections in the urban reach would enter the same aquifer system that the hiti depends on. The contamination cascade is not a theoretical concern: shallow groundwater contamination from informal urban drainage is already documented in Lalitpur.

**Implication for restoration design.** Restoration of canal-aquifer connectivity should be deliberate and selective. The upper reach (0–3 km), with confirmed clean perennial water and some stretches lined on one side only, can be allowed to recharge the aquifer beneficially. The middle and lower reaches, until sewage interception is complete, should be lined to prevent infiltration of contaminated flow. This is a design constraint on the Phase 3 urban-reach reconstruction (§10.4): impermeable lining is the appropriate default until upstream water quality is confirmed.

### 7.5 Climate resilience and monsoon variability

The canal's discharge is fundamentally rainfall-dependent. The Tikabhairav intake takes water from the Lele Khola, whose base flow reflects catchment recharge from the South Asian monsoon. Monsoon variability — increasing across the Hindu Kush Himalayan region — therefore translates directly into canal-discharge variability.

Two climate-related risks are relevant to restoration planning:

- **Inter-annual variability.** Drought years would produce reduced Lele Khola flow at the intake and less water delivered to the terminus. A restored canal sized to historical discharge may underperform in low-monsoon years.
- **Intra-seasonal extremes.** Increased frequency of extreme rainfall events would generate flash flows into the canal through its seven recorded cross-drainage points (§4.5) and through informal urban storm-water inputs. The canal's capacity to convey these extremes without bank failure has not been assessed and warrants attention in the Phase 1 cross-drainage re-assessment (§10.2).

**Implication for restoration design.** Design discharge assumptions should incorporate climate-informed projections rather than historical-average values. Intake rehabilitation should include provision for monitoring the Lele Khola's discharge at the intake over multi-year cycles. Cross-drainage structures should be sized for projected extreme events, not historical norms.

### 7.6 Cumulative environmental risk matrix

Synthesising the four risk categories across the do-nothing, restoration-without-measures, and restoration-with-measures scenarios:

| Risk category | Do nothing | Restore without measures | Restore with measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saptapatal Pokhari contamination | Medium (ongoing decline) | **Critical** | Low |
| Vector-borne disease risk | Medium (current stagnant reaches) | High (partial flow creates new pools) | Low (continuous flow) |
| Groundwater quality | Medium (current diffuse contamination) | High (contaminated canal infiltrates aquifer) | Medium (improving as sewage interception proceeds) |
| Climate-resilience risk | Latent (canal disused, no immediate exposure) | Medium (restored canal sized to historical Q) | Medium (mitigable through climate-informed design) |
| Cultural-heritage continuity | High (system decay) | Medium (partial recovery offset by pond contamination) | Low (system function restored coherently) |

***Table 7.1 — Cumulative environmental and public health risk matrix.***

The matrix establishes the policy core of this chapter: **the appropriate comparison is not "restoration vs. do nothing" but "restoration with parallel measures vs. restoration without parallel measures vs. do nothing".** Partial restoration without the parallel measures is the worst of the three options across most risk categories. The integrated phased plan of Chapter 10 is structured to ensure that no phase is delivered in isolation that would leave the system in this worst-case state.

### 8.4 Sensitivity discussion

The scoring framework is transparent, replicable, and useful — and it is not the only legitimate scoring framework that could be applied to the same data. Four sources of subjectivity should be acknowledged:

1. **Equal weighting across axes.** Contamination, silting and encroachment are scored on different scales (0–4, 0–2, 0–2) but contribute additively. A framework that weighted contamination more heavily, on the grounds that contamination is the most ecologically and politically consequential failure mode, would rank the clean but silted upper bands lower still.
2. **Coarse 1 km resolution.** Within-band variability is suppressed. A finer scoring (e.g. 250 m segments) would resolve the perennial-to-seasonal boundary at 3+124 m as a distinct intervention boundary, separating the clean upper part of the 3–4 km band from its contaminated lower part.
3. **Band-level aggregation.** Band scores are length-weighted means. Assigning each band its worst section (worst case) or its majority value would alter the composite scores.
4. **Uneven evidence.** Encroachment scores rest on field notes and POIs, which were not recorded systematically: a stretch with no note scores 0 even if it is encroached. The 3–4 km and 9–10 km bands rest on 683 m and 650 m of walked channel, and the last 1.77 km was not walked at all.

These caveats do not undermine the matrix's central output — that the 4–6 km and 7–8 km bands are the highest-priority intervention zones — but they should temper its use for fine-grained sequencing decisions. The matrix is appropriate for first-order prioritisation; final sequencing should be informed by site-specific engineering and stakeholder considerations beyond what the matrix captures.

### 9.1 Actor map

### 9.2 Jurisdictional boundaries

The canal alignment crosses two municipal jurisdictions, and the jurisdictional transition point is approximately at the **Chainage 5 km** boundary between the upper-alignment villages (under Godavari Municipality) and the lower-alignment urban areas (under Lalitpur Metropolitan City). The split falls inside the first critical band (4–6 km, §8.2), where sewage, storm water and waste dumping together begin at 4+417 m — meaning that the most ecologically damaging segment of the alignment is precisely the segment where coordinated cross-jurisdictional action is most needed and most difficult to organise.

Department of Irrigation maintains a national technical mandate for irrigation infrastructure under the relevant federal legislation, and was the agency responsible for the 2020 baseline study referenced throughout this report. DoI is a natural technical secretariat for any restoration framework but does not, on its own, have the urban-planning authority to address the encroachment dimensions of the problem; those are within the remit of GM, LMC, and DUDBC.

Department of Archaeology's involvement depends on whether the canal corridor is formally designated for heritage protection — currently it is not. A Department of Archaeology heritage classification, if obtained, would create a legal protection layer that constrains GM and LMC discretion on permissions affecting the corridor.

### 9.3 Legal and regulatory context

Three regulatory inconsistencies define the canal's current legal vulnerability:

1. **The 4 m buffer rule is in place but not enforced.** Building regulations across both jurisdictions notionally require a 4 m setback from the canal edge. The same regulatory framework has, in practice, permitted roads over the canal: the survey records it passing under a road at sections 65 and 67 (4+227–4+781 m) and near 3+441 m (POI 26), and running underground in eight further sections (Table 4.3). The rule and the road permissions are issued by overlapping authorities under the same legal framework.
2. **No formal heritage-corridor designation exists.** The canal is not currently classified as a heritage monument under Nepal's heritage legislation, despite its documented Licchavi-period status. Heritage classification would create legal grounds for buffer enforcement and would constrain further encroachment.
3. **No statutory water-users' association exists.** The traditional community body that maintained the canal has lapsed in most segments. There is no formal organisation with standing to represent water-users in restoration negotiations, to take on long-term maintenance responsibility, or to manage community-level disputes.

The combined effect is that the canal has neither the *de jure* legal protection that heritage classification would provide, nor the *de facto* community protection that an active water-users' association would provide. Restoration that did not address both of these structural gaps would face the same encroachment pressures that produced the current condition.

### 9.4 Community stewardship tradition

Traditionally, communities along the canal undertook annual pre-monsoon cleaning to maintain function: removing silt, clearing vegetation, repairing minor bank failures. The community labour was organised at the village level, supervised informally by elders, and timed to anticipate the seasonal flow surge. In the upper alignment (Tikabhairav, Chapagaon), traces of this practice persist; in the urbanised lower alignment, it has largely lapsed.

Three factors have weakened the tradition:

- **Loss of canal-dependent livelihoods.** Conversion of command-area agricultural land to housing has reduced the proportion of community members whose livelihoods depend directly on canal flow.
- **Anonymisation of contamination.** Sewage flowing into the canal from informal urban drainage means that physical handling of canal contents now carries health risk — a deterrent to volunteer labour.
- **Loss of supervisory structure.** The informal village-elder supervision has not been replaced by a formal water-users' association in most segments.

Re-activation of community stewardship is required for long-term sustainability of any restoration. The proposed institutional arrangement (§9.5) treats the formation of a recognised water-users' association as a precondition for transitioning from construction phase to maintenance phase.

### 9.5 Proposed institutional arrangement

A restoration framework adequate to the technical, governance, and community dimensions of the project would have the following structure:

- **Steering body: Tikabhairab Rajkulo Trust.** A formally constituted multi-stakeholder trust with rotating chair between Godavari Municipality and Lalitpur Metropolitan City; technical secretariat provided by the Department of Irrigation; observer status for the Department of Archaeology; ex-officio representation from CIUD and from the proposed Water Users' Association. The trust holds programme-level decision authority, including phasing, budget allocation, and dispute resolution.
- **Technical secretariat.** Provided by DoI. Holds technical standards, design oversight, and contractor management responsibility. Maintains the engineering documentation initiated by this report and by the recommended total-station survey.
- **Water Users' Association.** Statutorily constituted body representing communities along the alignment. Membership organised by ward; representation weighted toward currently active stewardship communities in the upper alignment. Holds maintenance responsibility from operational handover onward.
- **Legal corridor protection.** Department of Archaeology heritage classification of the canal corridor; statutory buffer-zone enforcement by GM and LMC; formal land-acquisition mechanism for resolving existing encroachments where rerouting is not feasible.

The proposed arrangement is not novel; it parallels institutional arrangements that have proved workable for comparable Kathmandu Valley heritage hydraulic restoration efforts. Its viability depends on political commitment from the two municipal governments, which is not assured.

## 10. Phased Intervention Plan

This chapter proposes a four-phase restoration sequence with cross-cutting institutional activities. The plan is **directional only**: no rupee cost figures are provided and detailed engineering specifications are out of scope for this baseline study. The plan's purpose is to establish a defensible sequence that addresses flow dependencies, environmental risks, and institutional preconditions in the right order.

### 10.1 Phasing logic

The sequence of restoration phases is determined by three constraints in this order of precedence:

1. **Flow dependencies.** Downstream interventions cannot be effective until upstream conditions support useful flow. Intake rehabilitation must precede or accompany downstream desilting; sewage interception must precede or accompany terminal pond connection.
2. **Environmental risk management.** No phase may be delivered in isolation that would create a worse environmental outcome than the do-nothing baseline. Specifically, restoring flow into the urban reach without parallel sewage interception is excluded as a phase boundary.
3. **Institutional preconditions.** Legal corridor protection, water-users' association formation, and steering body constitution must be in place before the first physical works begin, because their absence would expose the investment to the same encroachment pressures that produced the present condition.

Across the four phases, total construction duration is not specified (this is a function of funding flow and institutional capacity, both of which are out of scope). A conservative working planning horizon is 5–8 years from Phase 1 commencement to Phase 4 completion.

### 10.2 Phase 1 — Foundational works

**Scope.** Intake rehabilitation, sluice gate replacement, desilting of the perennial reach (0–3.1 km), and interception of the two wastewater entries in the first kilometre: the outflow at POI 1 (about 0+344 m) and the drain at section 14 (0+509–0+531 m, POI 4), where sewage was first recorded. The six cross-drainage points in the first 1.8 km (§4.5) are inspected and rehabilitated as needed.

**Flow objective.** Restore flow at the intake and keep the perennial reach clear. Silting was recorded in 45 of the 46 upper sections with a silting record, so desilting must be a routine activity, not a one-off. Protect the clean-water reach from its upstream contamination sources.

**Environmental objective.** Stop the wastewater entering upstream of the perennial / contaminated boundary at 3+124 m, ensuring that Phase 1 delivers clean flow into the upper canal.

**Key activities.** Structural assessment of intake works; weir desilting and re-grading; sluice gate replacement with operable mechanism and provision for stage / discharge monitoring; channel desilting downstream from intake to Chainage 3+124 m, and a desilting schedule for the upper reach agreed with the Water Users' Association; interception of the entries at POI 1 and section 14 by diversion to the nearest functional sewer or to small-scale on-site treatment; checks on the drains noted at section 40 (2+108–2+150 m); cross-drainage inspection.

**Institutional precondition.** Tikabhairab Rajkulo Trust constituted; DoI technical secretariat in place; corridor-protection legal status either confirmed or in formal progress; community engagement in upper-alignment villages active.

**Deliverable.** A 3.1 km reach of functional, clean, perennial flow from the intake to Chainage 3+124 m — the rehabilitable upper canal.

### 10.3 Phase 2 — Sewage interception

**Scope.** Interception of the wastewater entering the canal below 3+124 m: the drains at POIs 24 and 25 around the perennial-to-seasonal boundary, the two sewage lines at POIs 42 and 43 (about 6.9 km), and the drains feeding the 28 sections with sewage or waste recorded between 3+441 and 9+650 m. Includes the critical 4–6 km and 7–8 km bands identified in §8.2.

**Flow objective.** Remove the principal contamination sources from the middle alignment, enabling subsequent reconstruction of physically encroached lower sections without converting them into contamination conduits.

**Environmental objective.** Reduce the cumulative contamination load that would otherwise be carried to the Saptapatal Pokhari terminus. Address the worst-case "restore without measures" scenario identified in §7.6.

**Key activities.** Mapping of the drains and plots that feed the contaminated sections (the survey located only some of them, §5.4); design of interception infrastructure (combination of municipal sewer connections, decentralised treatment, and source-side household connections to formal sewer where available); construction of interception works; commissioning and monitoring.

**Institutional precondition.** Phase 1 complete (intake operational); KUKL / municipal sewer authority cooperation secured; community consent obtained for source-side interventions.

**Deliverable.** Known wastewater entries eliminated or routed to treatment; the middle-alignment urban reach no longer functions as a sewage conveyance channel.

### 10.4 Phase 3 — Urban reach reconstruction

**Scope.** Reconstruction of the underground and road-covered stretches (Table 4.3), and of the 2.1 km beyond 9+650 m that was not walked, as far as Saptapatal Pokhari. Treatment via covered-channel construction, horizontal directional drilling (HDD), or alternative routing on a stretch-by-stretch basis.

**Flow objective.** Restore continuous open or covered conveyance from the Phase 2 reach (downstream of the last interception point) through the urban core to the Phase 4 terminus.

**Environmental objective.** Restore the canal's spatial and ecological continuity; remove the worst encroachment-driven obstruction; create conditions for the canal to function as a designated heritage corridor rather than an invisible buried channel.

**Key activities.** Survey of the unwalked route beyond 9+650 m; stretch-by-stretch engineering analysis of the underground and road-covered stretches; selection of intervention type per stretch (covered channel within road reserve; HDD beneath road; surface re-routing along available adjacent corridor); detailed coordination with road authority and utility operators for each stretch; construction and commissioning.

**Institutional precondition.** Phase 2 complete (water quality acceptable for urban-reach flow); multi-authority coordination established (road authority, utility operators, LMC); land acquisition or easement secured where required.

**Deliverable.** Continuous canal conveyance — open or covered — from the intake to the Phase 4 terminus interface; no remaining buried segments without engineered conveyance.

### 10.5 Phase 4 — Terminus and pond integration

**Scope.** Final connection of the canal to Saptapatal Pokhari; construction of small-scale water-quality treatment immediately upstream of the pond; commissioning of the integrated rajkulo–pokhari system.

**Flow objective.** Restore the canal's original termination function as feeder to the pond; restore the historical hydraulic relationship between canal and pond.

**Environmental objective.** Ensure that water delivered to Saptapatal Pokhari meets the quality standards appropriate for a ritually used urban water body. Restore the cultural and hydrological function for which the system was originally designed.

**Key activities.** Detailed design of pond inflow structure (which must accommodate variable canal discharge without bank erosion of the pond); design and construction of small-scale treatment facility (likely a combination of settling and biological treatment, sized for the canal's measured discharge, §12.5); design of pond-level monitoring; ritual / community consultation with Saptapatal Pokhari Trust on appropriate inflow / handover arrangements; commissioning.

**Institutional precondition.** Phases 1–3 complete; water-quality monitoring confirms inflow standard achievable; Saptapatal Pokhari Trust consent obtained; long-term maintenance arrangement with Water Users' Association formalised.

**Deliverable.** The integrated rajkulo–pokhari system operating as designed; Saptapatal Pokhari receiving clean water; the canal restored to its historical function.

### 10.6 Cross-cutting activities

The four physical phases are accompanied by cross-cutting activities that run continuously from project commencement:

- **Legal corridor protection.** Department of Archaeology heritage classification of the corridor; formal statutory buffer zone enforcement by GM and LMC; encroachment register maintained.
- **Water Users' Association formation and capacity building.** Statutory constitution; ward-by-ward membership organisation; training in maintenance practice; preparation for operational handover.
- **Community engagement.** Continuous engagement with communities along the alignment, with particular attention to the urban-reach segments where canal awareness is weakest.
- **Monitoring and evaluation.** Hydrological monitoring at intake and terminus; water-quality monitoring at the intercepted wastewater entries and at the terminus; pond-level monitoring at Saptapatal Pokhari; periodic encroachment audits of the corridor.
- **Research follow-up.** Total-station survey (§12.1); epigraphic and archaeological work on the Tilmak question (§12.2); command-area mapping (§12.4); social survey (§12.6).

A condensed sequencing summary:

| Phase 2 | Phase 1 (intake operational) | Phase 1 functionally complete |

## 11. Constraints and Challenges

The constraints catalogued here are the residuals after the phased plan of Chapter 10 — the issues that remain even when restoration is sequenced correctly. They are organised by category to align with the institutional responsibilities in Chapter 9.

### 11.1 Construction challenges

The dominant construction challenge is **reconstruction of the underground and road-covered stretches** documented in Table 4.3. Each requires choices among covered channel construction, horizontal directional drilling, or rerouting, with the choice depending on stretch-specific factors including road category, traffic disruption tolerance, utility congestion in the road reserve, and adjacent land availability. The 2.1 km beyond 9+650 m, which was not walked, is the least known and potentially the most demanding: a new structure or alternative route may be needed to complete the connection to Saptapatal Pokhari.

Secondary construction challenges include **utility conflicts** at underground crossings (the alignment intersects water supply, drainage, telecommunications, and electricity lines), and **flow management** during construction — the lower alignment must continue to convey whatever flow it currently carries even during phased reconstruction.

### 11.2 Governance challenges

The multi-level authority structure described in §9.1 is the most consequential governance challenge. The canal's cross-jurisdictional path (Godavari Municipality / Lalitpur Metropolitan City) compounds with the Department of Irrigation's national technical mandate, the Department of Urban Development and Building Construction's regulatory remit, and the Department of Archaeology's potential heritage role to create a four-or-five-authority coordination problem.

The **policy inconsistency** identified in §9.3 — the 4 m buffer rule coexisting with permissions for road-over-canal construction — is fundamental. Until it is resolved through explicit legal protection of the canal corridor, further encroachments will continue to undermine restoration investment regardless of how well the physical works are executed.

**Political will and funding continuity** are uncertain. Restoration phases extend over multiple political cycles. Without an institutional framework (the Trust structure of §9.5) that survives political turnover, phase-to-phase continuity is at risk.

### 11.3 Maintenance challenges

The most fundamental maintenance challenge is the **decline of community stewardship**. Annual pre-monsoon cleaning has lapsed in most of the alignment; sustaining a restored canal requires its re-establishment, formalised through the proposed Water Users' Association (§9.5).

**Continuous desiltation** of the rehabilitated intake is required. One-time dredging at intake commissioning is not sufficient; routine silt removal — and, crucially, transport of removed silt away from the canal banks — must be a permanent operational activity.

**Sewage-source re-emergence** is a latent maintenance risk. The wastewater entries recorded in the survey (§5.4) represent the situation as of May 2022. As urbanisation continues, new inflows can emerge — particularly in the currently rural upper-alignment band — unless source-side household sewage management is implemented alongside canal-side interception. Continuous monitoring (§10.6) is required to identify and remediate new inflows before they become structural.

## 12. Research Gaps and Recommended Follow-Up

This survey establishes a baseline. It identifies six categories of follow-up work required before detailed restoration design can proceed.

### 12.1 Total station survey

**Priority: 1 (highest).** The absence of a level or total-station survey is the single most significant technical limitation of the present work: the bed slope of the canal is not known, section by section or on average along the walked channel.

**Required deliverable.** A continuous total-station survey from the Tikabhairav intake to the Lagankhel terminus, with bench mark establishment, longitudinal profile, and at least one cross-section per 100 m of alignment (denser in transition zones and at known break points).

**Implications of refined data.** The survey gives the per-section bed slope that any assessment of the canal's capacity to carry water and sediment requires. It also supports calibration of the discharge-stage relationships that Phase 1 monitoring will require.

**Estimated effort.** Two-person survey crew for approximately 3–4 weeks of field work depending on access conditions in the buried segments, plus 2 weeks of data processing.

### 12.2 Epigraphic and archaeological investigation — the Tilmak question

**Priority: 2.** The 'Tilmak' nomenclature in Licchavi-period inscriptions (§2.3) is anomalous and warrants investigation. Three lines of work are required:

1. **Systematic epigraphic review** of all Licchavi inscriptions referencing 'Tilmak', with attention to the contexts in which the term appears and any internal evidence about how the term was understood at the time of inscription.
2. **Archaeological investigation** of the subsoil stratigraphy at and immediately downstream of the Tikabhairav intake structure, capable of dating the earliest extant canal works through standard sub-surface archaeological methods.
3. **Comparative toponymic analysis** of other non-Sanskritic hydraulic names in Licchavi-period records, to establish whether 'Tilmak' is anomalous or part of a recognisable pattern.

Confirmation of pre-Licchavi origins would have substantial implications for heritage classification and the international funding sources available for restoration.

### 12.3 Quantitative water quality sampling

**Priority: 3.** Field observation has identified contamination categories but not concentrations. Quantitative laboratory characterisation is required to design appropriate sewage-interception and treatment infrastructure.

**Required deliverable.** A sampling programme covering the contamination points and wastewater entries of §5.4, the intake (clean baseline), and the Saptapatal Pokhari terminus. Parameters: BOD, COD, total suspended solids, faecal coliform counts, ammonia, nitrate, and heavy metals (lead, cadmium, chromium where industrial inflows are suspected). Seasonal repetition required (pre-monsoon, monsoon, post-monsoon, dry-season).

**Implications.** Quantitative loadings inform the sizing and treatment-process design of the Phase 4 terminus treatment facility, and the choice between source-side interception and end-of-pipe treatment in Phase 2.

### 12.4 Command area mapping

**Priority: 4.** The agricultural lands historically served by Tikabhairab Rajkulo were not systematically mapped in this survey. Identifying surviving agricultural parcels still hydraulically connected to the canal — and the households dependent on them — would quantify the irrigation benefit recoverable through restoration and provide the economic justification data required for funding proposals.

**Required deliverable.** A GIS-based command area map showing: parcels currently under cultivation; parcels with surviving canal connection; parcels converted to non-agricultural use; ownership and crop types; estimated water demand seasonally.

**Methodology.** Land-cover analysis from satellite imagery; field verification of canal-parcel connections; parcel-level interviews with surviving farming households.

### 12.5 Continuous hydrological monitoring

**Priority: 5.** Once the intake is partially rehabilitated, permanent monitoring infrastructure should be installed to characterise the canal's discharge behaviour across seasons and across multi-year climatic variation.

**Required deliverable.** Stage / discharge gauges at the intake, at the Chainage 3+124 m perennial-to-seasonal transition, and at the Saptapatal Pokhari terminus. Continuous logging with telemetry where feasible.

**Implications.** Provides the discharge data that this survey lacks; provides climate-resilience data per §7.5; informs Phase 4 treatment facility sizing.

### 12.6 Social survey

**Priority: 6.** Household-level data on communities along the alignment — current water uses, attitudes to restoration, willingness to participate in maintenance, exposure to current contamination — is required to design the community engagement and Water Users' Association elements of the restoration framework.

**Required deliverable.** A structured household survey covering communities in the seven settlements traversed (§4.1), with stratified sampling across ward-level units.

**Implications.** Provides the participation baseline against which Water Users' Association membership and stewardship activity can be planned.

## 13. Conclusion

The Tikabhairab Rajkulo presents a case in which the technical question and the institutional question diverge sharply. Physically, much of the canal survives. 81 of the 83 sections with a lining record are lined, and in May 2022 the first 3.1 km still carried water year-round, mostly of river quality. Below 3.4 km the picture changes: every section with a flow record was seasonal, 28 of the 34 with a water-quality record carried sewage or waste, and 1.73 km of walked canal could not be seen at all. The engineering problem is unblocking the intake, stopping the wastewater that enters the canal, reopening or replacing the underground and road-covered stretches, and building a small treatment facility upstream of the terminus pond. Whether the canal can carry the flow a restored system needs will not be known until its bed is levelled (§12.1). These are not small tasks, but they are bounded, definable, and within the standard repertoire of urban heritage hydraulic restoration.

Institutionally, the situation is harder. The canal crosses two municipal jurisdictions whose land-use policies have been mutually inconsistent; the legal framework that should protect the corridor has, in practice, permitted its burial; the community stewardship tradition that maintained the canal for centuries has lapsed under urbanisation; and the heritage classification that would constrain further encroachment has not been formally pursued. None of these gaps will close on their own. Closing them is a precondition for restoration, and they are addressed concretely in the stakeholder and governance framework of Chapter 9 and in the cross-cutting activities of Chapter 10.

The heritage dimension is unresolved and deserves the resources to resolve. The Tilmak question (§2.3, §12.2) cannot be settled by the field survey reported here. Three lines of work — epigraphic review, archaeological investigation, comparative toponymic analysis — are required. If the pre-Licchavi explanation is confirmed, Tikabhairab Rajkulo will rank among the oldest continuously utilised irrigation alignments documented in South Asia, with substantial implications for the international funding sources available for restoration. If it is not confirmed, the canal's Licchavi-period status and continuous-use heritage already justify the conservation framework recommended in this report. Either way the question is worth answering.

This report's specific recommendation to its sponsoring institutions is:

> Commission the **total-station survey** identified in §12.1 immediately as a stand-alone first step. Concurrently, initiate the institutional precondition work — Trust constitution, corridor legal status, Water Users' Association formation — that Phase 1 of the physical restoration depends on. The combined cost of these two parallel tracks is low compared to any phase of physical works, and their completion converts the present baseline study into an executable restoration design.

The path forward is well defined. What is now required is the institutional will to follow it.

## Appendix F — Recommended next-phase survey protocol

This appendix outlines the methodology recommended for the priority-1 follow-up activity (§12.1) — the total-station survey from intake to terminus.

### F.1 Objectives

To establish per-section bed slope with engineering-grade vertical accuracy; to establish a benchmark network suitable for downstream phase-by-phase construction control; to generate longitudinal-profile and cross-section data sufficient to support the Phase 1 intake design and the Phase 3 urban-reach reconstruction.

### F.2 Method

- **Equipment:** Reflectorless total station (e.g. Leica TS06, Trimble S5, or equivalent), tripod, prism set, GPS receiver for benchmark anchoring.
- **Benchmarks:** Establish a permanent reference benchmark at the intake (anchored on the DoI 2020 elevation 1,477.16 m) and at the terminus (DoI 2020 elevation 1,314.02 m). Establish intermediate temporary benchmarks every 1 km of alignment.
- **Profile:** Continuous longitudinal profile shots at not more than 25 m intervals along the canal centreline. Denser shots at structural transitions (intake works, cross-drainage structures, hume-pipe transitions, road crossings).
- **Cross-sections:** Minimum one full cross-section per 100 m of alignment. Denser cross-sections in the perennial reach (every 50 m, 0–3.1 km) and at the contamination points and wastewater entries of §5.4.
- **Buried sections:** Where access permits, profile through manhole access; where access does not permit, fall back to inference from upstream and downstream visible sections and flag the gap in deliverables.

### F.3 Deliverables

- Continuous longitudinal profile from intake to terminus, with per-section bed slope.
- Cross-section catalogue with at least 130 cross-sections.
- Updated chainage register cross-referenced to this report's Chainage values.
- Benchmark register with permanent reference monumentation.
- GeoPackage / DWG export for integration with the existing alignment GIS dataset.

### F.4 Estimated effort

Two-person survey crew, 3–4 weeks of field work plus 2 weeks of data processing and deliverable preparation. The underground and road-covered stretches (Table 4.3) and the 3.24 km not walked in this survey may require additional time depending on access arrangements with the relevant road authorities.
